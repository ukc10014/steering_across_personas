\begin{abstract}

LLM training may install traits either generally across personas or context-conditionally as personas are elicited during inference. We study the effect of constitutional training on persona-conditional trait representations (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, 8 traits, 10 personas/professions), using contrastive activation addition (CAA) and Open Character Training (OCT) LoRAs. We formalize a method to apportion changes in activations between shared and context-conditional terms, and find that most adapter-induced activation-space movement is shared across persona contexts, leaving a residual (about 3.6\% at layer 15) of the total measured change attributable to constitution-persona-trait interaction. We find qualified semantic specificity: the impulsiveness constitution gives disproportionate activation-magnitude changes on impulsivity and risk-taking, while misalignment shifts broadly across traits. Signed measures (logits) show a contrasting profile: both constitutions show large positive fitted offsets (log-odds at base-model indifference) on impulsivity and risk-taking. We also investigate constitutional training dynamics by localising the impulsiveness LoRA's effect, finding that 82\% of the final impulsivity-selectivity contrast emerges by epoch 0.48 of SFT on introspection data rather than the (earlier) DPO stage of OCT. These results suggest (a) character training can affect multiple traits, and (b) that the trait selectivity seems sensitive to process and measurement method, rather than to constitutional semantics alone.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%\paragraph{Question}
It is speculated that post-training, particularly reinforcement learning, might affect trait expression in context-dependent ways, allowing aligned and misaligned tendencies to coexist \citep{Betley2026SplitPersonas}. A training-induced trait change may install generally across contexts (personas/situations elicited at inference), or conditionally (appearing differently depending on context) \citep{Dubinski2026ConditionalMisalignment}. \citet{davies2026personas} operationalise this hypothesis, showing that trait representations in activation space are different depending on persona and on RL/post-training stage. 

We ask an analogous but narrower question: does constitutional character training \citep{maiya2025opencharactertrainingshaping} install trait representations conditionally upon contexts, or more generally across them? Secondly, which part of the OCT pipeline contributes most to changes in trait geometry?  We hypothesize that OCT installs a context-general update: for a given trait, a constitutional adapter changes the trait's representations in broadly similar ways across (persona/role) contexts. Note that, following \citet{davies2026personas}, we will use persona to mean  ``prompting context/system prompt" or ``professional role".

\paragraph{Contributions}
We make three empirical contributions. Firstly, we show that the effect of constitutional training is mostly shared across the tested occupational roles/personas, with a small three-way constitution $\times$ trait $\times$ persona interaction. Secondly, we find that measurement method matters when assessing semantic specificity: unsigned activation displacement measures and signed forced-choice logits give different trait selectivity profiles. Thirdly, for the impulsiveness constitution, we localize the emergence of its trait-selective logit phenotype to the first epoch of SFT upon DPO-generated introspection data, rather than as a direct result of the DPO weight update. During reproduction analysis of OCT results, we identified sensitivity to LoRA factorisation in the PEFT linear merge: this means functionally equivalent input adapters can produce different merged models. Finally, we made methodological contributions: a set of approaches to decompose activation-level changes into persona-common and persona-specific components; measures of geometry which are richer than cosine-to-null; controls using randomized LoRAs to partially distinguish possible effects of model displacement, from semantic effects of character training.

\section{Experimental Setup}
We apply the pipeline in \citet{davies2026personas}, to the open character constitutionally trained models in \citet{maiya2025opencharactertrainingshaping} (OCT). We use the OCT LoRAs published for Llama-3.1-8b-instruct in order to assess the geometry of context-dependent trait representations conditional on character: trait vectors calculated via contrastive-pair activation difference \citep{rimsky2024steering} for traits when the model has been in-context prompted to adopt a profession/role.  

\citet{davies2026personas} found that trait vectors clustered (low cosine-to-null distance) for certain roles and not others, and degree of clustering varied between traits. Our hypothesis was that under character training, trait representations might intensify or cluster (i.e. converge directionally, be amplified in magnitude, across personas), in a way that could be intuitively interpreted as “the effect of the constitution's semantic content on the representation”. Concretely, the impulsive OCT character should promote a more consistent representation of impulsivity across personas. Motivated by evidence that narrow fine-tuning can induce broader, correlated changes in model behaviour or persona \citep{Betley2026BroadMisalignment,chen2025persona}, we subsequently explored the possibility that some traits that seem intuitively adjacent to impulsivity, such as risk-taking, should also show a clustering under the impulsive OCT. We analyze these phenomena both through activations and using logit-based behavioral measures.

A principal object of study is $V_{c,t,p}\in\mathbb{R}^{4096}$ which denotes the trait vector under constitution/intervention $c$, trait $t$, and persona $p$. The adapter-induced change is $dV_{c,t,p}=V_{c,t,p}-V_{\mathrm{base},t,p}$, which can be conceptually decomposed into single factors and two/three-way interactions between factors (see also \ref{app:decomp}):
% \footnote{where $\mu$: overall/common shift, 
% \(C_c\): constitution-specific effect, 
% \(T_t\): trait-specific effect, 
% \(P_p\): persona-specific effect, 
% \((CT)_{c,t}\): constitution-by-trait interaction, 
% \((CP)_{c,p}\): constitution-by-persona interaction, 
% \((TP)_{t,p}\): trait-by-persona interaction, 
% \((CTP)_{c,t,p}\): genuinely constitution-specific modification of a particular trait in a particular persona.}

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:decomp}
\begin{aligned}
dV_{c,t,p}
={}&
\mu
+ \underbrace{C_c + T_t + P_p}_{\text{\footnotesize single-factor}}
+ \underbrace{(CT)_{c,t} + (CP)_{c,p} + (TP)_{t,p} + (CTP)_{c,t,p}}_{\text{\footnotesize interaction}}\\
={}&
\underbrace{\mu + C_c + T_t + (CT)_{c,t}}_{\text{\footnotesize context-general}}
+ \underbrace{P_p + (CP)_{c,p} + (TP)_{t,p} + (CTP)_{c,t,p}}_{\text{\footnotesize context-dependent}}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

In what follows, we primarily consider whether character-dependent change is general across personas (\(C,CT\)) or persona-dependent (\(CP,CTP\)); whether these changes bear some intuitive relationship to the semantics of the trained character; and where in the constitutional training process these changes emerge.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Character training mostly operates across personas generally}
\label{sec:ctp-tiny}
Constitutional character training does affect trait representations, but this effect is largely trait-specific and shared across personas, rather than varying as a function of both traits and persona. Specifically, Eq.\ref{eq:decomp} explains the variance in activation vectors between unadapted and adapted models as a sum of possible single-factor and interaction-between-factors effects. We implement this analysis of variance (ANOVA, details in App.\ref{app:anova}; components in Table~\ref{tab:anova_components}) on CAA vectors at L15/L20 to isolate the effect of constitutions via pairwise interactions, as well as the 3-way interaction between $CTP$. For L15 (App.~\ref{app:anova} and Fig.~\ref{fig:ctp}A), results are:$T$ 0.372, $TP$ 0.175, $CT$ 0.170, $\mu$ 0.124, $C$ 0.067, $P$ 0.043, $CTP$ 0.036, $CP$ 0.013. This can be summarised as: 71.4\% of the variance in representational change is in terms that have nothing to do with a specific constitution. Of the 28.6\% that depends on constitutions, the dominant component is the constitution's interaction with a trait (17\%), and this is mostly consistent across personas. About 3.6\% of variance that can be attributed to the three-way interaction of constitution, trait, and persona, with the caveat that untrained adapters show a higher value for this measure (3.6\%, 95\% CI 3.5--3.8\%, against 7.2\%, 6.8--7.7\%, for the untrained adapters).

\subsection{Trait profiles look different depending on CAA versus logit-based measurement}
\label{sec:trait-profiles}

Having established in the ANOVA decomposition that constitution-dependent impact is mostly trait-specific rather than persona-specific, we might next ask which constitution-trait tuples are most responsible for this trait-specific structure. The answer to this depends on the measurement method used. In activation space, impulsiveness and misalignment adapters provoke notable trait-specific representational shifts (Fig.~\ref{fig:trait-selectivity}A and reproduction with new seed for impulsiveness in App.\ref{app:impulsive-repro}). However, the two adapters give different results: Fig.~\ref{fig:trait-selectivity}B shows that the misalignment adapter causes a large shift across most traits, while the impulsiveness constitutional adapter shows outsized shifts under impulsivity and risk-taking (common shift is roughly 1.7--1.8$\times$ larger on risk-taking and impulsivity than on the other six traits).\footnote{Neither the mathematical nor goodness adapters showed comparable selectivity across measured traits. This seems unsurprising for the broadly-drawn goodness constitution; the mathematical adapter's selectivity is harder to interpret, since the trait set does not directly target mathematical or logical reasoning.}

However, as discussed in App.~\ref{app:signed}, activation-based results are unsigned: a large representational shift on the impulsivity trait cannot automatically be interpreted as ``the model has become more impulsive''. We therefore examined directional trait preferences (using forced-choice logits adjusted for the compression-towards-indifference that seems to result from any OCT LoRA). The quantity of interest is a fitted offset: the estimated trait-positive log-odds at base-model indifference under a linear calibration model (App.~\ref{app:signed}). It is not the same estimand as the average change in trait-positive answers, and the two can differ in sign. In Fig.~\ref{fig:trait-selectivity-logits}A, we see that both misalignment and impulsiveness characters have positive fitted offsets on the impulsivity and risk-taking traits, a different trait response profile than the CAA-based analysis above (which showed impulsiveness selectively affecting these two traits, and misalignment producing broader changes).

Finally, we found that correspondence between constitutional character and measured trait preferences is uneven: the loving character shows the (pre-registered/expected) positive fitted offsets on empathy and warmth. Sycophancy did not (for deference) (Fig.~\ref{fig:trait-selectivity-logits}A). We do not interpret whether the sycophancy result reflects training or a mismatch between the respective operationalizations of that character and deference.

\subsection{Constitutional behavioral phenotype emerges early in SFT}
\label{sec:results-sft}
Given the notable behaviour of the impulsivity character above, we next asked at which stage in the OCT pipeline (App.\ref{sec:oct-stage}) does the distinctive trait-selective behavioral expression (trait ``phenotype'') emerge, measured using output logits-based measures (relative trait preferences $B_1$, $B_2$ for impulsiveness and risk-taking over the other traits, defined in App.\ref{app:impulsive-paraphrase}). We map the full 3-epoch training trajectory for the impulsivity constitution, finding that the phenotype arises early in SFT, rather than DPO (82\% of the final effect $B_1$ by 0.48 epoch, and saturation by 0.70, see Fig.\ref{fig:oct-sft-curve}A). We control for the possibility that this is merely the unsurprising result of SFT perturbing the model more: when the application strength of a DPO-only adaptor is scaled such that it has the same functional effect on activations as the DPO + SFT training checkpoint, we find (Fig.\ref{fig:oct-sft-curve}D) a 5-8x trait-specific response for the SFT'd model. This confirms the trait-specificity of SFT, though DPO remains important as the generator of the introspection dataset. This result is limited to a single constitution, seed, and layer.

\subsection{Extending trait acquisition analysis to characters loving, goodness, sycophancy}
\label{sec:ext-trajectory}
In Sec.~\ref{sec:results-sft} we focused upon the impulsiveness character; now we extend the trait-instillation-timing analysis to loving, goodness and sycophancy. These are our reproduced training trajectories, not the stage checkpoints behind the released adapters, which are not public (App.~\ref{app:ext-trajectory}). Fig.~\ref{fig:ext-trajectory}A shows (y-axis) the fitted offset (trait-positive log-odds at base-model indifference; App.~\ref{app:signed}), pooled across the ten occupational personas, for each trait, against SFT epoch (x-axis), for the four constitutions. The first point on each curve is the completed DPO stage: the panels track subsequent SFT and do not resolve dynamics within DPO. Under impulsiveness, there is a weak positive offset (in the pre-SFT, DPO-only model) for all traits versus the base model. However, the offsets clearly differentiate through SFT. Loving and goodness show contrasting behavior where DPO installs differentiated trait offsets, which reorder moderately but remain broadly differentiated through SFT. Finally, sycophancy shows a profile and trajectory qualitatively similar to impulsiveness, albeit with confidence as the trait with the largest offset.

The bold line is a target contrast: $B_2$ (impulsivity and risk-taking jointly) for impulsiveness, and $\mathrm{LOV}$ (empathy and warmth jointly) for loving. It clearly rises with SFT for impulsiveness ($+0.32$ after DPO to $+2.01$ at 3 ep), but under loving it ends near its post-DPO level ($+0.82$ to $+0.78$; we have no paired interval for this change).

Panel B shows the timing with which the eight-trait offset profile changes during SFT: $q$ is progress along the eventual SFT-induced profile-change direction, measured from fitted offsets (definitions of $q$, $Q$ and $\tilde g$ are in App.~\ref{app:ext-trajectory}, Panel B). We see that progress along the final offset-profile-change direction reaches 71--94\% by half an epoch ($q$ at 0.48 ep, ten occupational personas: impulsiveness 0.80, loving 0.94, goodness 0.84, sycophancy 0.71; at about one epoch 0.91, 1.01, 1.01 and 0.82). Because $q$ is a projection, values slightly above one are possible; it is not a bounded percentage of character acquired, and it is a different statistic from the 82\% impulsivity-selectivity figure in Sec.~\ref{sec:results-sft}. The common-shift direction is $\geq 0.93$ aligned with its final direction by then (L15). However, as elsewhere in the paper, measurement method matters: a CAA-based geometric measure ($Q$, purple) does not follow the same trajectory for all characters. At about half an epoch (L15 point estimates), behavioural $q$ is ahead of geometric $Q$ for impulsiveness (0.80 against 0.64), loving (0.94 against 0.72) and goodness (0.84 against 0.73), while sycophancy reverses the ordering (0.71 against 0.77). These are descriptive comparisons between two measurements, not causal evidence. Both $q$ and $Q$ measure change since the completed DPO state; the persona-common share $S$ and magnitude $g$ in App.~\ref{app:ext-trajectory} measure total change from the unadapted base.

\subsection{Does an installed character persist through further character training?}
\label{sec:ext-xchar}
We next ran an exploratory, single-seed study of how continued character training affected an already fine-tuned model. A loving-trained model subjected to impulsiveness SFT (abbrev. loving$\rightarrow$impulsiveness), was compared to three controls: loving$\rightarrow$loving, base$\rightarrow$impulsiveness, base$\rightarrow$loving.\footnote{Note that ``base'' in this paper means ``no OCT training" i.e. not to be confused with a pretrained-only model.}. Each condition trains a fresh LoRA for one token-matched additional pass of SFT on an introspection corpus (released loving or base start, crossed with the impulsiveness or loving corpus).Qualitatively from Fig.~\ref{fig:ext-xchar}, we can see that loving$\rightarrow$impulsiveness trait profile moves in the same direction as base$\rightarrow$impulsiveness, albeit with smaller increases on impulsivity and risk-taking traits from a loving starting model (a moderation effect whose sign holds across the three estimators we checked but whose size varies by up to a factor of two; App.~\ref{app:xchar}). Approximately 86\% of the eventual increase in \(B_1\) from the loving starting model occurs within the first quarter of optimizer steps. Per-trait values are fitted forced-choice log-odds offsets relative to base (trait-positive log-odds at base-model indifference; App.~\ref{app:signed}); higher values indicate a larger offset towards trait-consistent answers. $B_1$ measures impulsivity selectivity relative to the other seven traits (Appendix~\ref{app:signed}).

Crucially, loving$\rightarrow$impulsiveness also substantially reduces empathy and warmth, retaining approximately 35\% and 45\%, respectively, of the starting loving model's fitted offsets relative to base. These principal increases and decreases keep their sign, with intervals excluding zero, under the reported estimator, a shared-slope variant and a raw polarity-balanced mean change (App.~\ref{app:xchar}). Further loving SFT left the LOV contrast essentially unchanged (within $\pm0.04$ under all three estimators) for the loving starting model. At the individual persona level, we note that impulsivity rises in all 11 personas on both starts, while empathy falls on the loving start in 10/11 personas and warmth in 9/11 (kindergarten teacher loses empathy but gains slightly on warmth), but the degree varies: therapist, kindergarten teacher and politician each keep about 75\% of their starting empathy offset (per-persona point estimates; their warmth ratios are 0.55, 1.03 and 0.70), against 9--27\% in five of the other seven occupational roles (drill sergeant 46\%; con artist gains). We interpret these results as partial replacement: under this protocol, additional SFT alters the model's trait phenotype by increasing certain trait offsets at the expense of others. Implementation details are in App.~\ref{app:xchar}.

\subsection{Open-ended behaviour: shared direction, role-dependent magnitude}
\label{sec:ext-behav}
As an open-ended behavioral check on the CAA/logit-based results above, we generated free-text answers to the 20 Persona Vectors \texttt{evil} evaluation questions \citep{chen2025persona}, but with the model conditioned upon each occupational persona. Answers were scored with that paper's malevolence and coherence judge rubrics (App.~\ref{app:ext-behav}). We use only its questions and judge; no persona-vector steering is applied. The misalignment adapter raised judged malevolence in all ten roles (mean $+37.6$ points on a 0--100 scale), but the size of the increase depended strongly on the role, from $+11.7$ for the therapist to $+62.8$ for the drill sergeant; the null (empty) context, a separate reference, rose by $+43.1$. Impulsiveness and goodness changed malevolence little, but base scores sit near zero in nine of ten roles, so this floor limits what either could show; the one resolvable decrease (goodness, drill sergeant) does not survive simultaneous correction. This shows that shared representational change might co-exist with differentiated output effects under different role prompts; a limitation is that this rubric scores overt malice only, and does not measure the eight traits referred to elsewhere in this paper. Lower scores in caring roles do not distinguish if the same harmful disposition was just expressed more politely, since severity and rubric sensitivity are entangled. Intervals are conditional on the 20 questions and the ten fixed roles.

\section{Discussion}

In considering implications for alignment, the results above show that a constitution's described character is insufficient to establish either the behavioural effects of training or their generality across contexts. For the characters tested, OCT's impact on traits appears to be largely context-general rather than persona-specific (the dominant effect of constitutional training is from the terms \(CT\), while \(CP\) and \(CTP\) are small), with dose-matched random perturbations reproducing much of the raw geometric movement. The loving and sycophancy results further showed that character-to-trait expectations were unevenly supported. 

Secondly, the choice of measurement method should distinguish between behavioural measures and residual-stream activation approaches, since these given different trait selectivity signatures, with the former being more informative for assessing directional effects. 

Lastly, character training should be assessed across the full pipeline, owing to complex and non-obvious interactions between stages. For the impulsiveness character, the trait-selective behavioural phenotype emerges mainly during SFT on a DPO-generated introspection corpus, rather than from the DPO weight update alone. In our reproduced trajectories for other constitutions (Sec.~\ref{sec:ext-trajectory}), the stage that carries the target contrast differs (for loving it is already present after DPO), so this should not be read as a general rule that SFT installs character. What SFT adds also differs: for loving and goodness, SFT increases the measured persona-common share of the trait-vector shift beyond an equally strong DPO update, while for impulsiveness the matched-dose excess is mainly behavioural. Larger persona-common share is a property of this activation geometry, not evidence of a more general psychological character.

\section*{Limitations}
\label{sec:limit}

Open-ended behavioral corroboration (Sec.~\ref{sec:ext-behav}) is for the misalignment adapter, on the malevolence rubric only. The extensions (Secs.~\ref{sec:ext-trajectory}--\ref{sec:ext-xchar}) use one training seed per arm, and their intervals are question-bootstrap (evaluation) uncertainty, not training-seed uncertainty. The stage trajectories are our reproductions, not the original runs behind the released adapters, and the loving reproduction under-recovers the released empathy and warmth offsets. In the cross-character study, repeating already-fitted loving data is a weak control: it does not match the size or novelty of an update on unfamiliar neutral data. The trajectory and cross-character activation analyses are now on one mask protocol, but activation comparisons that involve the released impulsiveness, goodness and sycophancy adapters (their reproduction fidelity, and released-versus-reproduction comparisons) still mix masks (App.~\ref{app:details}). Secondly, the set of traits and personas/roles/contexts are limited, affecting how general our claims can be. Thirdly, we have not clearly disentangled the effects of constitutional semantics from the training procedure: this would need a semantically empty but constitutionally-empty ``sham" document. Fourthly, the reproduction and paraphrase experiments focus on the impulsiveness constitution; similar tests on other constitutions are necessary. Our activation and logit measures are single-forward-pass readings with no generated reasoning, so they capture the model's immediate disposition on a forced choice, not what it would conclude after deliberating; because the SFT stage trains on extended reflective text, it may change deliberative behaviour in ways these measures do not detect. Only the open-ended behavioural check (Sec.~\ref{sec:ext-behav}) involves generated responses. Finally, the open issues in the App.~\ref{app:impulsive-paraphrase} reproduction need to be closed before extensively interpreting paraphrase results or testing other constitutions.

\section*{Ethical considerations}
This paper uses the misalignment adapters from \citet{maiya2025opencharactertrainingshaping}. Following the prior authors' practice, and owing to potential for misuse of those adapters, \newtext{we do not release any model trained from them.}

\paragraph{CAA details}
% Original paragraph, kept for reference until the red rewrite below is approved:
% CAA vectors are extracted using length-matched nonsense prompts and default/null role (i.e. corresponding to the "assistant persona" of \citet{lu2026assistant}). The primary measurement of interest is cosine similarity on the trait-extracted vector versus the vector extracted under null/assistant, at a mid/late layer (15, 20).
We used the repo underlying \citet{davies2026personas}, ported to a new model (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) and settled upon using the CAA method (versus the instruction variant (IV) that they referenced as a comparison). Details are LLM-summarised below:

For each trait we use 500 forced-choice scenarios (499 for empathy), each with one trait-positive and one trait-negative option; the positive option is (A) in 248 items and (B) in the rest. The persona description is the system prompt (one fixed wording per persona), the scenario and its two options form the user turn, and the assistant turn is a single answer letter. Each item is passed through the model twice without generation, once ending in the trait-positive letter and once in the trait-negative letter, and we record the residual-stream output of every decoder layer at the answer-letter token. The trait vector $V_{c,t,p}$ is the mean over items of the positive-minus-negative difference. Layers are indexed from zero, so L15 is the output of the sixteenth of 32 decoder blocks. We also extract vectors under an empty system prompt (``null'', the default assistant of \citet{lu2026assistant}) and under a length-matched nonsense system prompt; these are reference conditions and are not among the ten personas in any decomposition. For the logit-based measures, the same prompts are run without the assistant turn and we score the log-probabilities of the two answer letters at the first response position, giving the log-odds of the trait-positive over the trait-negative letter; the forced variant adds an instruction to answer with A or B. Neither measure involves generation or explicit reasoning: each is read from a single forward pass, and in the activation measure the answer letter is supplied rather than chosen by the model. Professional roles are farmer, politician, therapist, drill sergeant, street hustler, professor, tech CEO, kindergarten teacher, surgeon, con artist; traits investigated are assertiveness, empathy, risk-taking, honesty, confidence, deference, warmth, and impulsivity.

\paragraph{Attention mask}
The archived activations were extracted with an attention mask built from token identity. Llama-3.1 has no pad token, so the pad token is set to \texttt{<|eot\_id|>}, which also ends every chat turn; that mask therefore hid the genuine system- and user-turn terminators from attention. The logit measurements use a position-based mask and are unaffected. A diagnostic on 32 cells (base and impulsiveness models; impulsivity and honesty; three personas plus null; 150 questions; L15 and L20) compared the two masks. The corrected mask changed trait vectors less than resampling the question set does (cosine 0.97--0.996 between masks, against an extrapolated 0.59--0.91 between disjoint 500-question sets), moved the impulsiveness-minus-base gap by under 0.01, and preserved persona ordering in every cell. The base, the released adapters, the dose ladders and the untrained-adapter controls all use this legacy mask, so the main analyses (Secs.~\ref{sec:ctp-tiny}--\ref{sec:trait-profiles} and their appendices) are internally consistent. Later extractions, namely the impulsiveness reproductions, $P0$/$P1$, the stage states, the SFT curve and its dose-matched DPO states, the trajectory and dose-ladder states of App.~\ref{app:ext-trajectory}, and the cross-character arms, used the corrected mask. Extraction logs did not record the mask; this provenance is inferred from the runner code and extractor default at the time. As originally run, those analyses compared corrected-mask arms against a legacy-mask base. We have since re-extracted the unadapted base with the corrected mask (validated: same layout, provenance as expected, cell-mean cosine with the legacy base at least 0.997) and recomputed against it the trajectory geometry, functional doses and matched-dose comparisons (App.~\ref{app:ext-trajectory}), the cross-character activation selectivity (App.~\ref{app:xchar}), loving's reproduction fidelity, and the dose and selectivity of the impulsiveness reproduction, stage, SFT-curve and $P0$/$P1$ arms (for the SFT curve of Table~\ref{tab:oct-sft-curve}, dose changed by $-0.005$ to $+0.007$, selectivity by $-0.013$ to $+0.026$, and the dose-control ratios by at most 0.1). Against the mixed comparison, the actual base changed common magnitude $g$ by up to about 0.03, persona-common share $S$ by up to about 0.024, and functional doses by up to about 3\%, mostly at the weakly displaced DPO-only states and lowest ladder rungs; no qualitative statement changed. These changes are larger than an earlier synthetic-base estimate, which is superseded. Three groups of results should therefore be distinguished: the main analyses above, which are internally consistent on the legacy mask; the trajectory and cross-character analyses, now on the corrected mask throughout; and comparisons against the released impulsiveness, goodness and sycophancy adapters (their reproduction fidelity, and $A_4$ and released-adapter $B_3$ comparisons in App.~\ref{app:impulsive-paraphrase}), which still compare corrected-mask arms with legacy-mask released references because those released arms have not been re-extracted. The logit and open-ended measurements do not use these activations and are unaffected. One statistic remains unconfirmed: persona dispersion (App.~\ref{app:dispersion}) moved by 5--10\% relative on the diagnostic grid and has not been re-measured with the corrected mask.

\paragraph{Uncertainty}
Unless stated otherwise, intervals are 95\% question-bootstrap intervals: CAA questions are resampled with the same draws for every arm (so arm-to-arm comparisons are paired) and personas are averaged, not resampled. Geometry and common-shift measures use 200 replicates and logit measures 2,000; the joint matched-dose bootstrap of App.~\ref{app:ext-trajectory} uses 1{,}000. The intervals condition on these 8 traits and 10 personas and on one trained adapter per constitution, so they do not capture variation across training seeds.

\subsection{Decomposing CAA changes across factors}
\label{app:decomp}
We use the following conceptual decomposition of the change in CAA vector between adapted and unadapted models:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
dV_{c,t,p}
={}&
\mu
+ \underbrace{C_c + T_t + P_p}_{\text{\footnotesize single-factor}}{}
+ (TP)_{t,p}\\
&+ \underbrace{(CT)_{c,t} + (CP)_{c,p} + (CTP)_{c,t,p}}_{\text{\footnotesize constitution-dependent}}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Where:

$\mu$: overall/common shift, 
\(C_c\): constitution-specific effect, 
\(T_t\): trait-specific effect, 
\(P_p\): persona-specific effect, 
\((CT)_{c,t}\): constitution-by-trait interaction, 
\((CP)_{c,p}\): constitution-by-persona interaction, 
\((TP)_{t,p}\): trait-by-persona interaction, 
\((CTP)_{c,t,p}\): genuinely constitution-specific modification of a particular trait in a particular persona.

\subsection{Shared movement across personas}
\label{app:broad-shift}
In order to assess the degree to which constitutions have shifted representations in a persona-common way, for each constitution and trait, we average across the 10 personas, and compare the squared magnitude of the persona-averaged shift to the average across personas of the squared magnitude of the CAA shifts. This is shown in Fig.\ref{fig:trait-selectivity}.

Formulaically:
\[
dV_{c,t,p} = V_{c,t,p}-V_{\mathrm{base},t,p}
\]
and,
\[
dG_{c,t} = \tfrac{1}{P}\sum_{p}dV_{c,t,p}
\]
and for the common shift-across-personas share,
\[
\|dG_{c,t}\|^2\big/\mathrm{mean}_p\|dV_{c,t,p}\|^2
\]

At L15, the persona-common term accounts for 67--77\% of squared adapter-induced movement across trained arms. This result is consistent with the ANOVA decomposition in \ref{sec:ctp-tiny}.

\subsection{Calculating representational dissimilarity matrices (RDM)}
\label{app:rdm}
Although much of the representational shift is shared across personas, we find that there is a reorganization of the internal (between-personas) ordering of vectors, holding trait and constitution fixed. We construct a representational dissimilarity matrix (RDM), whose entries are the squared
Euclidean distances between persona-conditioned CAA vectors:
\begin{equation}
R_{c,t}(p,q)
=
\|V_{c,t,p}-V_{c,t,q}\|_2^2.
\end{equation}

For 10 personas, this is a symmetric $10\times10$ matrix with a zero diagonal and 45 unique off-diagonal distances. We measure RDM preservation using the Spearman correlation between these 45 distances in the adapted and base models:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\rho_{c,t}
={}&
\operatorname{Spearman}\!\big(
    \{R_{c,t}(p,q)\}_{p<q},\\
&\qquad\{R_{\mathrm{base},t}(p,q)\}_{p<q}
\big).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Higher correlation indicates greater preservation of between-personas arrangement: which persona pairs are relatively close together or far apart. Every RDM preservation values are raw Spearman correlation, with no correction for measurement reliability. 

\subsection{Measuring trait dispersion across personas}
\label{app:dispersion}

We are interested in how persona-conditioned trait vectors cluster or fan out around their own centroid, holding constitution fixed; the dispersion change $\mathrm{DR}_{c,t}$ measures how much this clustering changes between the unadapted and the character-trained model.

% Mean persona-conditioned vector for a given model and trait
\begin{equation}
\bar V_{c,t}
=
\frac{1}{P}\sum_{p=1}^{P}V_{c,t,p}.
\end{equation}

% RMS persona spread about that mean
\begin{equation}
S_{c,t}
=
\sqrt{
    \frac{1}{P}\sum_{p=1}^{P}
    \left\|V_{c,t,p}-\bar V_{c,t}\right\|_2^2
}.
\end{equation}

% Adapted-model RMS spread relative to the base model
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{DR}_{c,t}
=
\frac{S_{c,t}}{S_{\mathrm{base},t}}.
\end{equation}

It is well known in fields using statistical methods (e.g. neuroscience and machine learning) that when trying to estimate a variable's norm/variance from observational data, we must control for the fact that quadratic estimates of noisy data are systematically biased upwards. Following \citep{WaltherEtAl2016,WuEtAl2026}, one method of doing this is a split-batch cross-validated sampling-noise-debiasing. The basic idea is to split the noisy observational data into non-overlapping batches, estimate the relevant norm/variance independently on these disjoint subsets and replace the squared norm/variance of a single estimate (over the entire observations) with the cross-product of the two estimates (on the batches).

Concretely, in the simplified case, let the true vector-valued effect be
$\theta$, and let an estimate from one sample be

\[
\hat{\theta}^{A} = \theta + \epsilon_A,
\]

where $\epsilon_A$ is zero-mean sampling error. A naive estimate of the
squared norm of the effect is

\[
\left\|\hat{\theta}^{A}\right\|_2^2.
\]

Expanding algebraically,

\[
\left(\theta + \epsilon_A\right)^\top
\left(\theta + \epsilon_A\right),
\]

which gives

\[
\theta^\top\theta
+ 2\theta^\top\epsilon_A
+ \epsilon_A^\top\epsilon_A.
\]

Taking expectations,

\[
\mathbb{E}\left[
\left\|\hat{\theta}^{A}\right\|_2^2
\right]
=
\|\theta\|_2^2
+ 2\theta^\top\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_A]
+ \mathbb{E}\left[\epsilon_A^\top\epsilon_A\right].
\]

Assuming the sampling error has zero mean,
$\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_A]=0$, the linear error term vanishes, but the
quadratic noise term does not:

\[
\mathbb{E}\left[
\left\|\hat{\theta}^{A}\right\|_2^2
\right]
=
\|\theta\|_2^2
+
\operatorname{tr}\left(\Sigma_{\epsilon}\right),
\]

where

\[
\Sigma_{\epsilon}
=
\operatorname{Cov}(\epsilon_A).
\]

The split-batch debiasing method involves splitting the observations into two disjoint samples, producing two independent estimates,

\[
\hat{\theta}^{A} = \theta + \epsilon_A,
\qquad
\hat{\theta}^{B} = \theta + \epsilon_B.
\]

We estimate the squared norm using the cross-product

\[
\left(\hat{\theta}^{A}\right)^\top
\hat{\theta}^{B}.
\]

Expanding and taking expectations,

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}\left[
\left(\hat{\theta}^{A}\right)^\top
\hat{\theta}^{B}
\right]
&=
\theta^\top\theta
+ \theta^\top\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_B]\\
&\quad+ \mathbb{E}[\epsilon_A]^\top\theta
+ \mathbb{E}\left[\epsilon_A^\top\epsilon_B\right].
\end{aligned}
\]

If the errors are zero-mean and independent across the two disjoint
samples,

\[
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_A]
=
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_B]
=
0,
\qquad
\mathbb{E}\left[\epsilon_A^\top\epsilon_B\right]
=
0,
\]

and therefore

\[
\mathbb{E}\left[
\left(\hat{\theta}^{A}\right)^\top
\hat{\theta}^{B}
\right]
=
\theta^\top\theta
=
\|\theta\|_2^2.
\]

In this debiased example, the single-bias terms are zero (in expectation), but crucially, the cross product of bias terms from two non-overlapping estimates is \textit{also} zero in expectation, giving an estimate of $\|\theta\|_2^2$ that is unbiased.

In the present context, we have C=4,T=8,P=10,H=4096 corresponding to constitution, trait, persona/role, and residual stream size, and 500 questions. The data are split into 40 repetitions: disjoint batches of 250 questions each (repetitions reuse questions in different random partitions). The relevant code is here: \provlink{scripts/caa_three_way_interaction.py}.


\subsection{Functional dose and untrained-adapter controls}
\label{app:f-dose}
We find that constitutional LoRA adapters, though applied at an equal nominal application scale $s=1.0$, result in substantially different activation displacements. This means that simply perturbing the model more strongly causes progressively more distortion of the existing persona geometry. This means one must compare adapters using a measured ``functional dose" rather than nominal application scale.

We define a diagnostic of functional dose and as follows. 

At a fixed layer, let $V_{c,t,p}(s)$ denote the CAA trait vector
under adapter $c$ applied at scale $s$. We define mean relative
trait-vector displacement as
\begin{equation}
D_c(s)
=
\frac{1}{TP}
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{p=1}^{P}
\frac{
    \|V_{c,t,p}(s)-V_{\mathrm{base},t,p}\|_2
}{
    \|V_{\mathrm{base},t,p}\|_2
}.
\end{equation}

We express each adapter’s trait-vector displacement relative to the goodness adapter at \(s=1\), assigning that reference a functional dose of one.

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FD}_c(s)
=
\frac{D_c(s)}{D_{\mathrm{goodness}}(1)}.
\end{equation}

Thus, $\mathrm{FD}_{\mathrm{goodness}}(1)=1$ by construction.
This matched-dose diagnostic allows for comparisons between LoRAs. In order to functionally match doses (i.e. equalize the difference in activations to a character-trained model at a given layer), we need an adaptor application weight $s = 16-19$ to get the same effect as a trained OCT adaptor at $s = 1$. We also can see that functional dose affects the direction of $d_G$, as in Fig.~\ref{fig:rotation}. 

Next we look at dispersion (defined in \ref{app:dispersion}) amongst personas, and find that dispersion generally decreases with functional dose, although the dose–response curves differ between adapters (Fig.~\ref{fig:rdm-dose}B). At matched functional dose, all three untrained adapters reproduce most of the broad dispersion contraction, with misalignment the outlier showing stronger contraction than any untrained control (Fig.~\ref{fig:rdm-dose}D).

More interestingly, however, in Fig.~\ref{fig:rdm-dose}A, within-arm dose ladders show that RDM preservation (defined in App.~\ref{app:rdm}) falls as functional dose rises. Lower preservation indicates a change in which persona pair representations are relatively close together or far apart.  At matched functional dose (Fig.~\ref{fig:rdm-dose}C), goodness and impulsiveness follow essentially the same RDM-preservation estimates ($0.834$, $0.826$); mathematical sits above both ($0.878$) and misalignment below them ($0.768$). Geometrically, this means that ``mathematical is relatively ordering-preserving (amongst persona pairs) while misalignment is ordering-changing, while goodness and impulsiveness leave ordering relatively unchanged". 

One might (naively) attribute this geometric observation to the constitutions' content, interpreting some constitutions as preserving that arrangement and others as reorganizing it more substantially. In order to control for the possibility that any LoRA, including one devoid of semantic content, would cause a representational shift, we built untrained LoRAs, scrambled in three ways: random-IID weights, singular-spectrum-matched random directions, and per-module coordinate-permuted trained updates. We found that the apparent reordering above does not have an obvious semantic interpretation since untrained (by construction, semantically-null) arms straddled the whole trained range. In fact, at matched dose, \texttt{random\_iid} preserves geometry \emph{better} than every trained arm ($0.886$) and \texttt{random\_perm} preserves it \emph{worse} than misalignment ($0.761$).

The broad dose dependence also appears at layer 20, although the relative ordering of adapters changes (Fig.~\ref{fig:layer20}). This suggests that the dose effect is more consistent across layers than the adapter-specific preservation ranking.

\subsection{Corroborating CAA with logit-based behavioral evidence}
\label{app:signed}

There are two issues with the activation based measure in Fig.~\ref{fig:trait-selectivity}. Firstly, because we take the norm of $dG_{c,t}$ (persona-averaged change in the 4,096-dim CAA vectors for a given trait, constitution pair), the squaring operation eliminates directional/sign information. Therefore, we can only establish the size of a representational shift, not whether it has moved positively or negatively with respect to a trait direction or indeed whether it has moved orthogonally to it. So in the case of Fig.~\ref{fig:trait-selectivity}B, this would mean that a larger value for impulsivity simply means a larger relative persona-common change in the impulsivity CAA vector rather than increased impulsivity per se.

Algebraically, the primary object of interest in CAA is
\[
dV_{c,t,p} = V_{c,t,p}-V_{\mathrm{base},t,p}
\]
and,
\[
dG_{c,t} = \tfrac{1}{P}\sum_{p}dV_{c,t,p}
\]
the persona-common shift, and when we take a norm $\|dG_{c,t}\| = \|-dG_{c,t}\|$ destroying information on signed effect.

For the two panels in Fig.~\ref{fig:trait-selectivity},
\[
\|dG_{c,t}\|^2\big/\mathrm{mean}_p\|dV_{c,t,p}\|^2
\]
is the common-shift share, and
\[
\|dG_{c,t}\|\big/\mathrm{mean}_p\|V_{\mathrm{base},t,p}\|
\]
is the relative common shift. Obviously, in both cases, the unsigned numerator tells us nothing about whether quantities corresponded to an increase/decrease in a trait's expression strength.

In order to test whether representational shifts (between adapted and unadapted models) can be interpreted as “more” or “less” of a trait, the persona-common shift is projected onto the corresponding unadapted-model trait axis; this exercise can be repeatedly applied across a LoRA-strength ladder. One would check if a trait's projection retains the same sign, grows monotonically in absolute value, and extrapolates to a sufficiently small intercept at zero dose. We found that trained adapters largely fail these checks (Fig.~\ref{fig:signed}), whereas the randomized adapter passes for all traits. This means that we cannot interpret representational shifts as necessarily indicating semantic shifts: for example, the impulsiveness-trained model has not thereby become “more impulsive” simply because its impulsivity representation moves farther in one direction along the base-model trait axis.  

Given these issues with activation-based analysis, we assess the adapted model's trait preferences using a logit-based behavioural approach. The simplest way of doing this is to take the log of the output probability ratio between trait-positive and trait-negative answers, and then compare that between the adapted and unadapted model. However, this raw difference doesn't help identify a trait-specific bias, because we found that in adapted models, output log-odds move towards indifference (relative to the unadapted log-odds), making it unclear whether the adapted model's relative preferences in a given trait had actually changed (Fig.~\ref{fig:logits}A). 

To correct for this compression-towards-indifference, we perform regressions on the preference pairs for the adapted and the base models, which then separates out the actual shift in relative preferences from the compression component. 

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\log \frac{
    \Pr_{\mathrm{adapted}}(\text{trait+ answer}\mid i)
}{
    \Pr_{\mathrm{adapted}}(\text{trait- answer}\mid i)
}\\
&\quad= a + k \log \frac{
    \Pr_{\mathrm{base}}(\text{trait+ answer}\mid i)
}{
    \Pr_{\mathrm{base}}(\text{trait- answer}\mid i)
}
+ \varepsilon_i .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here, $i$ indexes evaluated question--persona items, $k$
measures retention of itemwise baseline log-odds across question--persona observations (question and persona variation together, not specifically persona-to-persona variation), and the intercept $a$ is our quantity of interest: it is the estimated trait-positive log-odds at base-model indifference under this linear calibration model, and is plotted on the vertical axis of
Fig.~\ref{fig:logits}C. Positive values favor the
trait-positive answer at base indifference; negative values favor the
trait-negative answer.

\textbf{Answer polarity.} The trait-positive option is (A) in some items and (B) in others, so that a preference for one letter, independent of content, is not mistaken for a trait preference. We fit the regression separately for the two polarity groups and average the two intercepts and slopes. Writing the base log-odds as $x=u+bs$ and the adapted log-odds as $y=a+k_s u+cs$, with $s=\pm1$ for the polarity and $b,c$ the base and adapted letter biases, the averaged intercept is $a+b(k_-{-}k_+)/2$. The adapted model's letter bias $c$ therefore cancels, but the base model's letter bias cancels only when the two polarity slopes are equal. The calibration is model-based: for the trajectory and cross-character states (not the released-adapter panels in this appendix), a CPU sensitivity analysis compared this estimator with a shared-slope model including a polarity term, and with a raw polarity-balanced mean change; the implied letter-bias term was small (median 0.02, maximum 0.10 log-odds).

\textbf{Offsets and mean changes are different estimands.} The fitted offset $a$ is not the average change in trait-positive log-odds, and the two can differ in sign. At the endpoint of our reproduced loving trajectory (App.~\ref{app:ext-trajectory}), the empathy offset is approximately $+1.50$ while the polarity-balanced mean log-odds change from base is approximately $-0.43$; for warmth they are $+1.22$ and $-0.74$. The base model already strongly prefers the empathetic option on average, and the adapted model retains only about half of each item's baseline log-odds, so the shift at base indifference is positive even though the average item's log-odds falls. Neither metric is universally better, but statements in this paper that a constitution ``raises'' a trait relative to base should be read as claims about the offset at base indifference, not about average choice behaviour.

\textbf{Support near indifference and estimator sensitivity.} The offset is a prediction at $L_{\mathrm{base}}=0$, so it depends on how much data lies near zero and on whether the linear calibration holds over the range of the data. Table~\ref{tab:offset-robustness} reports both for the forced prompt of Fig.~\ref{fig:trait-selectivity-logits}. Support near zero is thin: the base model's mean absolute log-odds is 3.3 to 5.3 depending on the trait, and only 4 to 10\% of question--persona items lie within $\pm0.5$ of indifference (237 to 563 items per trait; 19 to 42\% within $\pm2$). The intercept is therefore partly an extrapolation from items on which the base model already has a clear preference. We tested how much this matters with two estimators that relax the linear model. The first is a quadratic calibration (the intercept of $L_{\mathrm{adapted}}$ on $1$, $L_{\mathrm{base}}$ and $L_{\mathrm{base}}^2$, fitted per polarity group and averaged as above). The second uses no calibration model: it takes the polarity-balanced mean of adapted log-odds over items with $|L_{\mathrm{base}}|<\delta$, for $\delta=0.5,1,2$. Across the 48 released-adapter offsets (six adapters by eight traits), the quadratic intercept is within 0.08 of the linear one (median 0.02), and the local estimators are within 0.34 (median 0.05 to 0.06), with the largest gaps on assertiveness, the trait with least support. The trained adapters' own-target contrasts in Fig.~\ref{fig:trait-selectivity-logits}B move by at most 0.17 across the five estimators (impulsiveness on impulsivity $+2.12$ to $+2.28$; loving on empathy and warmth $+1.64$ to $+1.81$; sycophancy on deference $-1.05$ to $-1.08$), and none changes sign. The untrained controls are less stable: random-perm on impulsivity ranges from $-0.78$ to $-1.29$, and random-iid on empathy and warmth changes sign ($-0.11$ linear, $+0.12$ at $\delta=0.5$). Under the default prompt the estimators disagree more (quadratic within 0.16 of linear; local within 0.50, largest for loving on empathy, $+2.86$ linear against $+2.36$ local), but the own-target contrasts again keep their sign. The principal trained-adapter contrasts on the forced prompt are therefore robust to the alternative estimators tested, while default-prompt offsets carry an estimator dependence of up to about 0.5 that their bootstrap intervals do not include. Two diagnostics are not yet reported: $R^2$ and residual dispersion within each polarity group, and persona-specific intercepts, which would show whether the pooled offset is stable across personas. Both need the per-item logits, which were not re-read for this analysis.

\label{app:impulsive-paraphrase}

\subsection{Setup}

The objective here was to understand where the trait selective behavioural phenotype emerges within the OCT training pipeline and whether that is sensitive to rephrasings of the impulsiveness constitution. Before doing this, we needed to reproduce the original OCT setup in \citep{maiya2025opencharactertrainingshaping} in order to have a baseline.

Constitutional training in the OCT recipe \citep{maiya2025opencharactertrainingshaping} has multiple stages: in a distillation stage, a teacher model prompted with the constitution generates preferred responses, which are paired with student-generated responses as rejected alternatives for DPO training of a student LoRA. This (DPO'd) student then generates a training corpus from reflections upon, and conversations with, other instances of itself. The introspection corpus is then used for supervised fine-tuning (SFT) of the DPO'd student to produce a second LoRA. The DPO and SFT LoRAs are merged, and this is the released artifact. No reasoning trace reaches the student at either stage. The teacher (GLM-4.5-Air) is a reasoning model whose generation is prefilled with the opening of a reasoning block that restates the constitution's traits, but OCT keeps only the text after \texttt{</think>} as the preferred response and discards the reasoning. The introspection corpus is ordinary assistant text written by the student (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, which does not emit a separate reasoning trace); it is generated under a system prompt listing the traits, and that system prompt is dropped from the saved training examples, so SFT teaches the student to produce the reflective text without the trait list in context. We examine the contributions of these stages as well as the effects of the merge below.

Below, the DPO-adapted model is $M_D$, the model with both full DPO and SFT updates is $M_{D+S}$, and the PEFT-merged model is $M_F$. As a separate control, $M_S$ is the unmodified model SFT-trained  on the same DPO-generated introspection corpus. Thus, $M_S$ excludes the DPO weight update but retains its influence through the training data.

For this investigation, we quantify (for a given trait(s)) a behavioural signature using two signed trait-selectivity contrasts: impulsivity relative
to the other seven traits ($B_1$), and impulsivity and risk-taking
jointly relative to the remaining six traits ($B_2$).
Following the forced-choice output-logit approach described above
(see App.~\ref{app:signed}), we calculate these measures as follows:

\[
L
=
\log
\frac{P(\text{trait-positive answer})}
     {P(\text{trait-negative answer})}.
\]

\[
L_{\mathrm{adapted}}
=
a_t + k_t L_{\mathrm{base}} + \epsilon,
\]

\[
B_1
=
a_{\mathrm{impulsivity}}
-
\frac{1}{7}
\sum_{t \neq \mathrm{impulsivity}} a_t.
\]

\[
\begin{aligned}
B_2
={}&
\frac{
a_{\mathrm{impulsivity}}
+
a_{\mathrm{risk\text{-}taking}}
}{2}\\
&-
\frac{1}{6}
\sum_{t \notin
\{\mathrm{impulsivity},\,\mathrm{risk\text{-}taking}\}}
a_t.
\end{aligned}
\]

The intuition is: for each constitution and each trait, we calculate the logprob ratio (measuring relative preference between trait-positive and trait-negative answers) from the unadapted and the constitutionally adapted model. These preference measures are plotted: the preference of the unadapted model (x-axis) versus the adapted model (y-axis). The regression's fitted y-intercept, $a_t$ tells us that: where the unadapted model would be indifferent, the adapted model has fitted log-odds $a_t$. A positive fitted offset at base-model indifference ($a_t$) means a bias towards the trait-positive answer after accounting for the fitted rescaling of existing preferences; it is not an average change in trait-positive answers. The regression is fitted separately by answer polarity and the intercepts averaged, which removes the base letter bias only if the two polarity slopes are equal (App.~\ref{app:signed}). The $a_t$ figures are fed into the formulas above in order to assess the adapted model's preference on a target trait relative to its preferences for others.

The above measures $B_1$, $B_2$ are signed logit-based contrasts, while $B_3$ (below) is an unsigned activation displacement selectivity ratio (like the CAA-based measures above) that looks at how much more the two target traits (impulsivity, risk-taking) move when compared to the six non-targeted traits.

\[
g_t
=
\frac{\lVert dG_t\rVert}
{\frac{1}{P}\sum_{p=1}^{P}
\lVert V^{\mathrm{base}}_{t,p}\rVert},
\]
\[
dG_t
=
\frac{1}{P}\sum_{p=1}^{P}
\left(
V^{\mathrm{adapted}}_{t,p}
-
V^{\mathrm{base}}_{t,p}
\right).
\]

\[
B_3
=
\frac{
\frac{1}{2}
\left(
g_{\mathrm{impulsivity}}
+
g_{\mathrm{risk}}
\right)
}{
\frac{1}{6}
\sum_{t\notin
\{\mathrm{impulsivity},\mathrm{risk}\}}
g_t
}.
\]

Finally, $A_4$ is the constitutional adapter's overall (i.e. averaged across all personas and traits) trait-vector displacement relative
to the released OCT impulsiveness adapter:
\[
A_{4,c}
=
\frac{D_c(1)}{D_{\mathrm{OCT}}(1)},
\]
where $D_c(1)$ is the mean relative trait-vector displacement
defined in App.~\ref{app:f-dose}, and $\mathrm{OCT}$ denotes
the released impulsiveness adapter. Both adapters are evaluated
at application scale $s=1$, using the same layer and evaluation
items. The released adapter therefore scores one; values above
one indicate greater mean displacement, and values below one
indicate less. This relative activation-based measure is distinct from, but complements, the ANOVA analysis attribution above in \ref{app:anova}.

\subsection{Explaining the P0 discrepancy}

We did not do a comprehensive forensic audit of the OCT pipeline, but suggest several possible sources for the discrepancy. Firstly, as stated in App.\ref{app:impulsive-repro} even when using LoRAs generated using OCT's published data for the introspection corpus, we couldn't fully replicate the effect of the merged OCT adapter.

There was also an infrastructure issue: the teacher model (GLM-4.5-air), which generates the preference data for the distillation/DPO stage, is hosted through OpenRouter's API. The original OCT implementation included a prefill to the teacher prompt with the start of a reasoning trace (see App. A of \citep{maiya2025opencharactertrainingshaping}). Our hosted setup did not allow for this assistant-side prefill, and when we supplied it, it was interpreted as a completed prior assistant turn. Therefore, we omitted the prefill. 

How big a deal was this? We're not sure -- our approach obviously was able to produce a downstream SFT phenotype, but it wasn't as strong as in the original OCT work. However, we can't tell how this deviation changed the DPO training distribution, and how much of the discrepancy should be attributed to the prefill issue, the items below, or something else.

We then considered the complexities of the multi-stage character training process, particularly how LoRAs are merged. For each targeted model weight matrix $W$, a rank-$r$ LoRA parameterises the update as
\[
\Delta W = sBA, \qquad s=\alpha/r,
\]
where $A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ and
$B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r}$, with the LoRA rank $r=64$ and model dimension 4,096.  Let the DPO and
introspection-SFT updates be
\[
D=s_D B_DA_D,\qquad S=s_S B_SA_S.
\]

OCT's released adapter is constructed using PEFT's
\texttt{add\_weighted\_adapter} with weights $w_D=1$ and $w_S=0.25$
and \texttt{combination\_type="linear"}.  For positive weights this
combines the LoRA factors:
\[
A_F=
\sqrt{w_Ds_D}\,A_D+\sqrt{w_Ss_S}\,A_S,
\]
\[
B_F=
\sqrt{w_Ds_D}\,B_D+\sqrt{w_Ss_S}\,B_S.
\]

Here $r=64$ and $\alpha=128$ for both component adapters, so
$s_D=s_S=2$. Hence
\[
A_F=\sqrt{2}A_D+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}A_S,
\]
\[
B_F=\sqrt{2}B_D+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}B_S,
\]
and therefore
\[
\begin{aligned}
\Delta W_F
&=B_FA_F\\
&=2B_DA_D+\frac{1}{2}B_SA_S+B_DA_S+B_SA_D\\
&=D+0.25S+B_DA_S+B_SA_D.
\end{aligned}
\]

\citet{baines2026personacartography}, who reuse this merge for their own trait adapters, independently note that it combines the low-rank factors rather than summing the weight deltas, and so introduces cross terms. We hypothesize, but do not definitively prove, that the cross-terms $B_DA_S + B_SA_D$ in the factor-space merge might amplify the above differences between our and the original OCT implementation. Specifically, if these 2 matrices are aligned, it can be shown that the net effect resembles a re-weighting of the DPO or SFT stages (potentially increasing the LoRA's contribution to the model's weight matrix). The reason these matrices might be aligned for any fixed LoRA adapter is that LoRA factor decomposition is not unique; empirically we found the factor coordinates were seed-dependent. 

The table below shows that with the same seeds, the $A_D$ and $A_S$ matrices were roughly collinear, and the functional effect $B1$ was much higher under a PEFT merger than an additive merger. When we choose a different seed for DPO (leaving SFT's seed untouched), the two A matrices became approximately orthogonal, and the PEFT merger and the simple addition gave similar $B1$.

We then made a causal intervention: for the SFT adapter, we replaced each of the matrices $A_S, B_S$ with its negative. Since the matrices are multiplied, that should have had no effect on the final weights or behavior of the model. However, when submitted to the PEFT factor merge, we found a behavioral change in $B1$ from +1.923 to -0.729. This indicates that two (apparently) functionally identical pairs of input adapters can produce radically different final weight matrices under the PEFT factor space merge.

It is important to note that the failure of this $P0$ replication is unlikely to be solely a result of factor-space merging peculiarities, since the reproduction and the $P0$ matrices were aligned. However, the point of this discussion is to show that PEFT LoRA merge is highly sensitive to factor-space representation, which suggests upstream discrepancies (one possibility being the prefill issue) in training might have been magnified.

\subsection{Paraphrase result}

This appendix was motivated by an experiment that tested effects of paraphrasing the impulsiveness constitution. However, given the reproduction discrepancy above, we now treat the impulsiveness paraphrase as an exploratory investigation, provided for completeness. We lightly paraphrased the impulsiveness constitution (diff'd here: \provone{oct_variants/impulsiveness_paraphrase/traits.diff}), and then follow the published OCT recipe (albeit with the quirks of our reproduction): training data generation for DPO using GLM-4.5-air on OpenRouter; introspection corpus creation; SFT of the DPO'd model on the corpus; and PEFT merger of the resulting LoRAs. We measured the $B1$, $B2$ changes between the P1 (paraphrase) and the P0 (failed reproduction) as +0.092 and +0.096 respectively (CIs of [+0.038, +0.144], [+0.058, +0.132]). These gaps are much lower, and opposite in sign, than the reproduction gap calculated above (between P0 and the fixed data reproduction) of -0.642. 

The relatively small movement in overall trait contrast ($B1$, $B2$) does not mean that the trait profile was stable. The P1-P0 gap was significant for off-target traits: honesty +0.632, confidence -0.312, deference -0.268, assertiveness +0.272 versus the changes for impulsivity (+0.123) or risk-taking (+0.105).

This was a single exploratory comparison, but it suggests a possible takeaway with both measurement and semantic aspects: a light paraphrase can present an apparently stable overall contrast ($B1$, $B2$) while substantially reorganizing the detailed pattern of traits.

As discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:ext-trajectory} and elsewhere in this paper, the chosen measurement method (CAA-based versus log-odds-based) leads to somewhat different pictures of SFT trajectories. Figs.~\ref{fig:ext-trajectory} (panels A--B) and~\ref{fig:ext-trajectory-full} (panels C--D) visualize these differences based upon the following calculated quantities.

Let $c$ index constitutions, $t$ the eight traits, and $p$
personas. Let $e=0$ denote the DPO-only state and $F$ the
final SFT checkpoint. For the geometric quantities,
$P=10$ occupational personas. Superscript $S$ refers to post-DPO training. Behavioural log-odds regressions
and CAA-based geometric summaries both use the ten occupational personas, and an overbar to denotes an
unweighted mean over the eight traits.

\noindent\textbf{Panel A: fitted trait-preference profiles.}
For each trait and checkpoint, we follow the method in Sec.~\ref{app:signed}:
 the forced-choice regression
pools (over personas) question items $i$:
\begin{equation}
L_{c,t,i}(e)
 = a_{c,t}(e)+k_{c,t}(e)L_{\mathrm{base},t,i}
   +\varepsilon_i,
\end{equation}
where $L$ is the log-odds of the trait-positive over the
trait-negative answer. To account for answer-letter preferences, regressions are fitted separately for questions where option A expresses the trait and questions where option B expresses it; the two fitted intercepts are then averaged (see App.~\ref{app:signed} for when this cancels letter bias). $a_{c,t}(e)$ is the offset at base-model indifference, not a mean change in trait-positive answers.
The coloured curves show $a_{c,t}(e)$; the black curves
show the following contrasts where specified:
\begin{align}
B_{2,c}(e)
 &= \frac{a_{c,\mathrm{impulsivity}}(e)
          +a_{c,\mathrm{risk\text{-}taking}}(e)}{2}
    -\frac16\sum_{t\notin
       \{\mathrm{impulsivity},\mathrm{risk\text{-}taking}\}}
       a_{c,t}(e), \\
\mathrm{LOV}_c(e)
 &= \frac{a_{c,\mathrm{empathy}}(e)
          +a_{c,\mathrm{warmth}}(e)}{2}
    -\frac16\sum_{t\notin
       \{\mathrm{empathy},\mathrm{warmth}\}}a_{c,t}(e).
\end{align}
$B_2$ and $\mathrm{LOV}$ are the trait-profile measures for the characters impulsiveness and loving, respectively; no analogous contrast is shown for goodness or sycophancy. These contrasts summarise the target trait pairs used in the preceding analyses (Fig.~\ref{fig:trait-selectivity-logits}).

For panels B--D, define the total and incremental
representational changes (covering the DPO-through-SFT and within-SFT portions of training, respectively):
\begin{align}
dV_{c,t,p}(e)
 &= V_{c,t,p}(e)-V_{\mathrm{base},t,p}, \\
dV^{S}_{c,t,p}(e)
 &= V_{c,t,p}(e)-V_{c,t,p}(0), \\
dG_{c,t}(e)
 &= \frac1P\sum_p dV_{c,t,p}(e), \\
dG^{S}_{c,t}(e)
 &= \frac1P\sum_p dV^{S}_{c,t,p}(e).
\end{align}
Define the baseline scale and relative common magnitude:
\begin{equation}
b_t=\frac1P\sum_p\|V_{\mathrm{base},t,p}\|,
\qquad
g_{c,t}(e)=\frac{\|dG_{c,t}(e)\|}{b_t},
\qquad
\bar g_c(e)=\frac18\sum_t g_{c,t}(e).
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Panel B: normalised trajectory progress.}
Let $\mathbf a_c(e)=
(a_{c,1}(e),\ldots,a_{c,8}(e))^\top$
collect the eight fitted trait offsets, and define
$\mathbf d_c(e)=\mathbf a_c(e)-\mathbf a_c(0)$.
\begin{align}
q_c(e)
 &= \frac{\mathbf d_c(e)^\top\mathbf d_c(F)}
          {\|\mathbf d_c(F)\|^2}, \\
Q_c(e)
 &= \frac18\sum_t
    \frac{dG^{S}_{c,t}(e)^\top dG^{S}_{c,t}(F)}
         {\|dG^{S}_{c,t}(F)\|^2}.
\end{align}

In words, $q_c(e)$ measures progress from the DPO-only
trait profile along the direction of its final SFT-induced
change. Consider that $d_c(e)$ is an 8-trait vector (a difference of log-odds intercepts between training checkpoints), and we want to measure the alignment/magnitude of that vector (at a checkpoint) with the corresponding vector at the end-of-training. That is, how much movement has the change-in-trait vector suffered as a result of training, expressed as a proportion of its final change. $Q_c(e)$ presents an analogous measure based upon CAA. The intuition of Fig.~\ref{fig:ext-trajectory}B is simply that the measurement method gives different trajectories, but we do not offer a further interpretation. Neither $q$ nor $Q$ is a percentage of character acquired; as projections, either can exceed one. Both now use the ten occupational personas. An earlier version of $q$ pooled eleven personas including null (0.76, 0.85, 0.83 and 0.70 at 0.48 epoch, against 0.80, 0.94, 0.84 and 0.71 on the matched set).

The dashed orange curve in Panel B shows
\begin{equation}
\widetilde g_c(e)
 = \frac{\bar g_c(e)-\bar g_c(0)}
        {\bar g_c(F)-\bar g_c(0)}.
\end{equation}
It measures the increase in common-shift magnitude since DPO as a fraction of its final increase.

\noindent\textbf{Panel C, D: persona-common squared shares, magnitude, direction (supplemental)}
A central question in this paper has been how trait representations change, under constitutional training, on a persona-common rather than persona-conditional basis. Panel C compares accumulated changes from the unadapted base with additional changes from the DPO-only state; Panel D describes the magnitude and direction of the
accumulated persona-common shift from base.



In Panel C, DPO-through-SFT and SFT-incremental shares are
\begin{align}
\bar S_c(e)
 &= \frac18\sum_t
    \frac{\|dG_{c,t}(e)\|^2}
         {P^{-1}\sum_p\|dV_{c,t,p}(e)\|^2}, \\
\bar S^{S}_c(e)
 &= \frac18\sum_t
    \frac{\|dG^{S}_{c,t}(e)\|^2}
         {P^{-1}\sum_p\|dV^{S}_{c,t,p}(e)\|^2}.
\end{align}

The orange curve in Panel D shows
\begin{equation}
\bar g_c(e)
 = \frac18\sum_t
   \frac{\|dG_{c,t}(e)\|}
        {P^{-1}\sum_p\|V_{\mathrm{base},t,p}\|}.
\end{equation}
It measures the magnitude of the persona-common shift
from the unadapted base, relative to the baseline
trait-vector scale, averaged across traits.

The green curve shows the changing alignment (rotation, calculated using mean cosine similarity) of the shift in persona-common vector (at a checkpoint) compared to the total change over base$\rightarrow$SFT training.
\begin{align}
\bar\gamma_c(e)
 &= \frac18\sum_t
    \cos\!\left(dG_{c,t}(e),dG_{c,t}(F)\right) \\
 &= \frac18\sum_t
    \frac{dG_{c,t}(e)^\top dG_{c,t}(F)}
         {\|dG_{c,t}(e)\|\,\|dG_{c,t}(F)\|}.
\end{align}

\noindent\textbf{Estimation.} The equations above define the estimands. A squared norm estimated from noisy vectors is biased upwards, because sampling noise adds squared magnitude even when the true change is small. We therefore compute squared norms such as $\|dG_{c,t}\|^2$ as the inner product of estimates from two disjoint question halves, which removes this bias in expectation. When the true signal is weak such an estimate can fall below zero; this does not mean the squared norm is negative, only that the data cannot resolve a small positive value. Because these quantities are the denominators of $Q$, $\bar S$, $\bar S^S$ and $\bar\gamma$, we withhold a ratio rather than divide by an unreliable denominator: a per-trait ratio is reported only if its point denominator is positive and at least 97.5\% of bootstrap replicates are positive, and an eight-trait mean only if every trait qualifies. The 97.5\% threshold is a reporting rule we chose, not a statistical requirement. At L15 every reported quantity qualifies. At L20 some per-trait means are not estimable (for impulsiveness the incremental direction and $Q$ at every SFT checkpoint; for sycophancy the total and incremental directions and $Q$ at every non-final state); an energy-pooled ratio, which sums energies across traits before dividing, is defined there but is a different estimand and is not substituted. An earlier version of this analysis floored the denominator at a small positive value, which gave invalid intervals at L20; that version is superseded. The trajectory figures show L15 only.

\noindent\textbf{Matched-dose comparison.} This control asks whether SFT does something different from DPO, or only moves the model further. For each SFT checkpoint we scale the same constitution's DPO-only adapter until its measured functional dose (App.~\ref{app:f-dose}) matches the checkpoint's (the ``ladder''), and report the excess, checkpoint minus scaled DPO, of a geometry quantity or a behavioural contrast (Fig.~\ref{fig:ext-dose}). Zero means no difference on that measure; a positive excess means SFT produces more of it than equally displaced DPO, and a negative excess less. The \emph{endpoint excess} is this difference at the final SFT checkpoint (three epochs, the trained model). Uncertainty comes from a joint paired bootstrap with 1{,}000 replicates. Each replicate resamples the questions once per trait and uses that resample for the base, every trajectory state and every ladder rung; it then recomputes the doses, re-matches the checkpoint's dose by linear interpolation within that replicate's ladder, and takes the difference, so the interval includes uncertainty in the matching itself. An excess is reported only if the checkpoint's point dose lies within the ladder and at least 97.5\% of replicates are defined (dose bracketed and ratio denominators positive); otherwise both the estimate and its interval are withheld. A withheld value means we compute the comparison but deliberately report no number, because it fails the reliability rule set out just before it. Intervals are pointwise, describe question-sampling uncertainty only, and do not reflect variation across training seeds. We do not quote intervals for the doses themselves, which are plug-in statistics with noise bias.

Three comparisons fail this rule, so we report no value for them (they are tagged gaps in Fig.~\ref{fig:ext-dose}). First, at the sycophancy L15 endpoint the SFT model has moved slightly further than the strongest scaled-DPO model we trained (dose 0.6700 against a top rung of 0.6695), so there is no equally displaced DPO model to compare it with, and every L15 endpoint excess for sycophancy is withheld rather than extrapolated; at about one epoch, where a match exists, the L15 $S$ excess is $+0.027$ $[+0.016,+0.086]$. Second, for goodness at L20 and 0.24 epoch the match exists at the point estimate, but in 53\% of bootstrap resamples it falls outside the ladder, so the comparison is too unstable to report. Third, the L20 per-trait-mean $S$ excess for sycophancy is withheld at every state because one trait's denominator (deference) is unreliable in too many resamples (95.1\% defined, below the 97.5\% threshold). The per-trait-mean and energy-pooled $S$ weight traits differently and can disagree in sign: for impulsiveness at 0.48 epoch (L15) they are $-0.007$ $[-0.010,-0.000]$ and $+0.011$ $[+0.002,+0.023]$. We treat the per-trait mean as the primary summary, for consistency with $\bar S$ in the trajectory figures, and report the energy-pooled version as a sensitivity check. The impulsiveness case shows why the choice of summary matters. Restricted to its two target traits, the $S$ excess is positive at every checkpoint at both layers (at one epoch, $+0.044$ $[+0.035,+0.055]$ at L15 and $+0.114$ $[+0.088,+0.158]$ at L20), so a per-trait mean near or below zero at L15 implies that SFT makes the change on the six other traits slightly less persona-common than matched DPO does. The per-trait mean turns positive at L20 mainly because the target-trait excess is about twice as large there. Impulsiveness SFT therefore concentrates persona-common structure on its target traits rather than raising it uniformly.

Endpoint excesses (ten personas, L15) are summarised in Sec.~\ref{sec:ext-trajectory}. Per-trait behavioural excesses at the endpoint (L15, ten personas) are: impulsiveness impulsivity $+1.79$, risk-taking $+0.96$, confidence $+0.59$, honesty $+0.39$, assertiveness $-0.54$; loving assertiveness $-1.09$, confidence $+0.58$, impulsivity $+0.54$, risk-taking $+0.34$, honesty $-0.29$; goodness confidence $+0.57$, deference $-0.34$, empathy $-0.30$, warmth $-0.29$. Pooling eleven personas including null, as in an earlier version of this analysis, gives a $B_2$ excess of $+1.46$ $[+1.40,+1.53]$ and a LOV excess of $-0.01$ $[-0.08,+0.06]$; we report these only as a population sensitivity.

\section{Details on cross-character SFT}
\label{app:xchar}
The setup was similar to the experiments above: two models (the unadapted/base Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, or the OCT-released loving-adapted model); subjected to two training corpora (an impulsiveness introspection dataset, or an loving introspection datasets) for a total of 4 training conditions. Both corpora use released introspection examples; the impulsiveness corpus reuses the exact prepared file from our earlier fixed-data reproduction (App.~\ref{app:impulsive-repro}). We did not do any new DPO (as noted above, the OCT released adapter was produced through DPO, and the introspection dataset was generated by DPO'd student). Each condition trained a fresh LoRA (\(r=64\), \(\alpha=128\), zero-initialised \(B\), targeting all attention and MLP projection matrices), and the SFT training was a single pass token-matched (4.48 million supervised tokens; impulsiveness 328 steps, loving 367 steps) with measurement checkpoints at 25/50/100\% of optimizer steps (impulsiveness steps 82/164/328, loving 92/184/367; a replay of the trainer's sampler places the first two at about 25\% and 50\% of supervised tokens). In order to reuse existing training infrastructure, we re-created the ``loving-adapted model'' (OCT only provides LoRA adapters, not a fully merged model) by folding the released loving LoRA into an unadapted model. We then trained the relevant SFT LoRA (impulsiveness or loving), which was added onto that (folded and frozen) starting model.

The 256-row held-out tail of each corpus is excluded from this pass; for the loving start it is part of the released loving corpus, so it is not necessarily unseen by the released model. The original run manifests recorded the impulsiveness corpus hash for every run, including the loving-corpus runs; the training inputs themselves were verified to be correct, and the corrections are recorded in \texttt{docs/runs/oct/xchar\_*.provenance\_correction.json}.

\textbf{Estimator sensitivity.} Table~\ref{tab:xchar-estimators} compares the reported polarity-split offset (App.~\ref{app:signed}) with a shared-slope model including a polarity term and with a raw polarity-balanced mean change. The four principal changes from the loving start after impulsiveness SFT keep their sign under all three estimators, each with an interval excluding zero; the estimators differ in size by at most 0.13. The moderation term keeps its sign but not its size. For loving$\rightarrow$loving, the LOV contrast stays within $\pm0.04$ of the start under all three, while individual empathy and warmth changes are small ($|\Delta|\le0.11$) and change sign between estimators.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}

\paragraph{General notes:}
This section has had significant LLM assistance in auditing and documenting artefacts. All paths in Table~\ref{tab:provenance} are relative to the code release at \url{\provbase}. Table~\ref{tab:provenance} gives, for every figure, the script that draws it and a CSV of the exact values it plots. Those CSVs are written by the same scripts from cached analysis outputs under \path{outputs/analysis/}, so every number in every figure is auditable without rerunning inference. No figure in this paper requires a forward pass to rebuild. The whole set is CPU-reproducible from the archived analysis outputs.

\paragraph{Models and artifacts:}
The underlying model we used was \texttt{meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct}, revision \texttt{0e9e39f249a1}. Released OCT adapters: \texttt{maius/llama-3.1-8b-it-personas} (goodness, mathematical, impulsiveness, loving, sycophancy) and \texttt{maius/llama-3.1-8b-it-misalignment}; frozen introspection data for the fixed-data reproduction from \texttt{maius/OpenCharacterTraining-data}. Our adapters (fixed-data reproduction, seed 123456; second seed, 987654; $P0$; $P1$; and the separate DPO and SFT stage LoRAs) will be released with a later version of this paper.\footnote{An exception is the misalignment LoRA, which will be withheld in keeping with the original OCT researchers' practice.} Each training run records the upstream OCT commit (\texttt{d1da9f03}, with local modifications), the OpenRLHF fork commit, and package versions in a per-run manifest under \provlink{docs/runs/oct/}. \newtext{Code: \url{https://github.com/ukc10014/steering_across_personas}; this version of the paper corresponds to commit \texttt{[COMMIT]}.} Training runs (reproduction, second seed, $P0$, $P1$ and the stage-specific adapters) uses three epochs of introspection SFT, matching the released adapters. Note: this differs from the OCT repository which specifies one epoch; that change (commit \texttt{bd20b87}) was made after the released adapters were uploaded. 

\paragraph{Measurement:}
Activations: \provlink{pipeline/2c_caa_activations.py}; logits: \provlink{pipeline/2d_caa_logits.py}; questions: \provlink{data/prompts/caa/}; personas: \provlink{data/personas/}. Prompt construction, token positions and the attention mask are described in App.~\ref{app:details}. Layers for extractions were roughly middle-layers (15 and 20 (zero-indexed)), motivated by prior layer sweeps showing effective behavioural steering at intermediate depths \citep{rimsky2024steering,chen2025persona}.

\paragraph{Controls and estimators:}
Untrained adapters: \provlink{scripts/make_random_lora.py}; functional dose: \provlink{scripts/functional_dose.py}; common shift: \provlink{scripts/common_shift.py}; RDM and dispersion: \provlink{scripts/geometry_analysis.py}; three-way decomposition with cross-fitting (40 disjoint half-splits, 200 bootstrap replicates, seed 0): \provlink{scripts/caa_three_way_interaction.py}; compression regression and logit contrasts (2,000 replicates): \provlink{scripts/caa_logits_analysis.py}; $B_3$: \provlink{scripts/selectivity_score.py}.

\paragraph{Environment:}
Training: the OCT OpenRLHF fork (\texttt{maiush/OpenRLHF}) with torch 2.8.0+cu128, transformers 4.57.0, deepspeed 0.18.0, peft 0.20.0, accelerate 1.14.0 and flash-attn 2.8.3.post1. Measurement: torch 2.8.0+cu128 and transformers 4.57.6, matching the version used for every released arm. The PEFT version matters for the merge results in App.~\ref{app:impulsive-paraphrase}. Python dependencies: \provlink{pyproject.toml}.
