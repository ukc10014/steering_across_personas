\title{Trait Representations Conditional on Persona Context and Constitutional Training}

\author{Anonymous authors}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}

In prior work on persona representations, trait activations have been found to vary based on professional role context. How does this context-dependent trait representation vary under constitutional training? We test Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct over 8 traits and 10 roles, using contrastive activation addition (CAA) at middle layers, using 4 published LoRA adapters from the Open Character Training (OCT) dataset. We find that constitutional training mostly reshapes the CAA vector space generically across role contexts. After controlling for this persona-common movement, we find a small, constitution-role-trait component remains (about 3.6\% of the total measured change). The impulsiveness (and to a lesser extent misalignment) characters specifically produce disproportionally large changes in the representation of impulsivity. For the impulsiveness constitution, this effect also generalises to the semantically-adjacent trait of risk-taking. These residual stream observations were confirmed in logits, with substantial moves towards impulsivity/risk-taking under these constitutions, versus minimal change on controls (goodness/flourishing and mathematical constitutions). For impulsiveness, much of the representational change is traced to the SFT (on introspection data) stage of OCT. If verified on a broader range of traits, roles, and rewordings of constitutions, these results would support a view that constitutional character training produces semantically-intuitive changes in model representations and behavioural inclinations, superimposed on much larger generic training effects.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\paragraph{Question}
Existing research on persona-role interactions show that trait representations in activation space (abbrev. ``trait representations") are different depending on context, role or persona \citep{anonymous2026personas}.\footnote{The ``persona" concept \citep{marks2025psm}, although a useful framing, remains underspecified and might refer to a range of phenomena in activation or weight space \citep{chen2025persona,lu2026assistant}; behavioural observations; instance-level representations, rather than a basin of attraction stable across contexts or instances \citep{beckmann2026minds}; or the emerging results of various training stages \citep{minder2026spp}. At the scope of this paper's analysis, and following \citet{anonymous2026personas}, the use of "persona" should be understood as ``prompting context/system prompt" or "professional role".} Does constitutional character training \citep{maiya2025opencharactertrainingshaping} differentially reinforce trait representations and if so, how clearly can we distinguish whether that representational change is a surface-level or deeper content-level? If OCT constitutional training affects representations, where precisely in the post-training pipeline contributes most to changes in trait geometry?  We hypothesise that character training instills a deep orientation within the model and that trait representations are more likely to track the character training, rather than the specific context/persona/role the model is placed in.

This paper's contributions are in part methodological: a set of approaches to decompose activation-level changes into persona-common and persona-specific components; measures of geometry (dispersion and rerepresentational dissimilarity matrices (RDMs)) which are richer than cosine-to-null; controls for the varying functional efficacy of OCT adapters; and partial controls through 3 randomization approaches. We also show initial results that constitution $\times$ trait $\times$ persona interaction is present, but relatively small compared to geometry changes shared across personas. We find that, for the impulsiveness constitution, representational changes are substantially introduced in the first epoch of SFT upon introspection data (generated by a LLM character-trained via DPO), rather than as a direct result of the DPO training.

\section{Experimental Setup}
We apply the pipeline in \citet{anonymous2026personas}, to the open character constitutionally trained models in \citet{maiya2025opencharactertrainingshaping} (OCT). We use the OCT LoRAs published for Llama-3.1-8b-instruct in order to assess the geometry of context-dependent trait representations conditional on character, we apply the pipeline in \citet{anonymous2026personas}: trait vectors calculated via contrastive-pair activation difference \citep{rimsky2024steering} for traits when the model has been in-context prompted to adopt a profession/role. \footnote{CAA vectors are also extracted using length-matched nonsense prompts and default/null role (i.e. which should correspond to the "assistant persona" of \citet{lu2026assistant}). The primary measurement of interest is cosine similarity on the trait-extracted vector versus the vector extracted under null/assistant, at a mid/late layer (15, 20).} \footnote{Professional roles are farmer, politician, therapist, drill sergeant, street hustler, professor, tech CEO, kindergarten teacher, surgeon, con artist; traits investigated are assertiveness, empathy, risk-taking, honesty, confidence, deference, warmth, and impulsivity. OCT constitutional adapters tested are: goodness (aka "flourishing"), mathematical, impulsiveness, misalignment} 

\citet{anonymous2026personas} found that trait vectors clustered (low cosine-to-null distance) for certain roles and not others, and degree of clustering varied between traits. Under character training those representations could converge directionally, or be amplified in magnitude, and these effects could differ by role/context/persona. We expected a clustering of representations, which could be intuitively interpreted as “the effect of the constitution's semantic content on the representation”. For example, the impulsive OCT character should promote a more consistent or clustered representation of impulsivity across roles. In addition, some traits that seem intuitively adjacent to impulsivity, like, say, risk-taking, should also show clustering.

Let $V_{c,t,p}\in\mathbb{R}^{4096}$ denote the trait vector under constitution/intervention $c$, trait $t$, and persona $p$. The adapter-induced change: $dV_{c,t,p}=V_{c,t,p}-V_{\mathrm{base},t,p}$, which can be conceptually decomposed as:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:decomp}
dV_{c,t,p}
=
\mu
+ C_c
+ T_t
+ P_p
+ (CT)_{c,t}
+ (CP)_{c,p}
+ (TP)_{t,p}
+ (CTP)_{c,t,p}.
\end{equation}

where $\mu$: overall/common shift, 
\(C_c\): constitution-specific effect, 
\(T_t\): trait-specific effect, 
\(P_p\): persona-specific effect, 
\((CT)_{c,t}\): constitution-by-trait interaction, 
\((CP)_{c,p}\): constitution-by-persona interaction, 
\((TP)_{t,p}\): trait-by-persona interaction, 
\((CTP)_{c,t,p}\): genuinely constitution-specific modification of a particular trait in a particular persona.

\paragraph{Key statistics we measure, all compared to an unadapted model.}
We measure role-common shift $dG_{c,t}$, which could be result of using a LoRA adaptor, or the specific constitutional training, or other aspect of training/model architecture shared across all constitutions and roles: $\mathrm{mean}_p\,dV_{c,t,p}$. Trait representation geometry is characterized using dispersion (mean squared distance of persona-conditioned trait vectors from their within-trait centroid) and RDM (for every pair of personas, hold constitution and trait fixed, giving a $10\times10$ matrix for 45 unique pairwise distances). The functional efficacy or ``dose'' of an adapter is quantified: vary adaptor weight $s$ such that change in activations of the adapted vs unadapted models, at a given layer, is normalised to some target. The primary item of interest is $C\times T\times P$: residual ANOVA-style interaction after lower-order constitution, trait, persona, and pairwise terms are removed.

In Appendix \ref{sec:oct-stage}, we measure trait-selective behavioural expression from output logits, using impulsivity as the primary target and impulsivity plus risk-taking as a secondary contrast, to localise where in the constitutional training pipeline the target phenotype emerges. We complement this with activation-space measures of the relative magnitude of shifts in the target traits and the cosine alignment of those shifts with the final trained model.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Impulsiveness and misalignment constitutions show specific adapter-induced movement}

Most of the representational impact of constitutional OCT is shared across personas (App.\ref{app:broad-shift}, \ref{app:global-reshaping}), accounting for over 77\% of squared adapter-induced movement in CAA vector at L15. After controlling for these generic effects, however, we find that adapters for impulsiveness and misalignment show the highest common shifts in Fig.~\ref{fig:trait-selectivity}A. This behaviour was confirmed for impulsiveness through a reproduction using the released seed, and a second optimisation seed (App.\ref{app:impulsive-repro}).

The two adapters give different results: Fig.~\ref{fig:trait-selectivity}B shows that the misalignment adapter causes a large shift across most traits. On the other hand the impulsiveness constitutional adapter is more discriminatory, showing outsized shifts under impulsivity and risk-taking (common shift is roughly 1.7--1.9$\times$ larger on risk-taking and impulsivity than on the other six traits: $1.72\times$ at L15, $1.87\times$ at L20).

We also tested the dispersion of CAA trait representations (around their centroid), and the relative ordering of the 8 traits (App.\ref{app:rdm} and Fig.~\ref{fig:rdm-dose}). These results don't seem to have a clear semantic interpretation, in part because the controls (untrained adapters) required large application weights $s$, which itself distorts the trait geometry.


\subsection{$C\times T\times P$ effect is tiny}
\label{sec:ctp-tiny}
In Eq.\ref{eq:decomp} we consider the variance in activation vectors between unadapted and adapted models as being a sum of possible single-factor effects and interactions between factors. We do an split-batch debiasing analysis of variance (ANOVA, details in App.\ref{app:anova}) on CAA vectors at L15/L20 to assess the degree to which variance can be decomposed into a common component $\mu$ (representational change shared across all $CTP$ for whatever reason, including training pipeline), components specific to the 4 constitutions, 8 traits and 10 personas, and then pairwise interactions, and finally, a 3-way interaction between $CTP$. For L15 (and Fig.~\ref{fig:ctp}A), results are:$T$ 0.372, $TP$ 0.175, $CT$ 0.170, $\mu$ 0.124, $C$ 0.067, $P$ 0.043, $CTP$ 0.036, $CP$ 0.013. This can be summarised as: 71.4\% of the variance in representational change is across terms that have no constitutional dependency. 28.6\% is constitution-dependent. This analysis of variance means that the semantics of a constitution does have an effect, but mostly via traits at 17\%. Importantly, after lower-order terms are removed, only about 3.6\% of trained L15 adapter-induced squared change is specific to the particular $CTP$ triple.

There are several caveats here: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item An untrained adapter shows a higher $CTP$ residual (Fig.~\ref{fig:ctp}B). ``Untrained" here means 64-rank LoRA adapters that have been scrambled (via IID randomisation, spectrum-matching, and coordinate-permuted, relative to an OCT constitution in the latter two cases)) but match the statistical signature of the adapters from OCT \citep{maiya2025opencharactertrainingshaping}, but scaled to an application strength that is functionally equivalent to the OCT LoRA's effect at the respective layer (measured using average activation norm). This might be interpreted as making the measured $CTP$ of 0.036 (above) suspect. 
    \item This randomisation itself has its own caveat: the high application strengths (up to $s{=}32$) needed for the functionally-matched untrained adapters pushes the model towards incoherence on neutral prompts. The output distribution changed substantially: changed the argmax token at \(48\)--\(69\%\) of positions, and spot-checking a log shows incoherent generation at $s{=}32$.
    \item The results above show that constitutional training causes representational changes, but (per App.\ref{app:signed} and Fig.\ref{fig:signed}) we didn't find that CAA-based activation changes on an adapted model projected reliably to the corresponding trait vector of an undapated model, such that we could say the LoRA adapter correlated with "more" or "less" of a given trait.
\end{itemize}

The caveats in \ref{sec:ctp-tiny} motivated a different approach (ratio of output logits on CAA prompts, controlled for the general compression of preferences that constitutional LoRAs install in the model, see App.\ref{app:signed} and Fig.\ref{fig:logits}) which *did* show a directionally-signed effect of constitutional training.

\subsection{Constitutional behavioral phenotype emerges early in SFT}

As discussed in App.\ref{sec:oct-stage}, OCT involves DPO, SFT, and PEFT merging stages; at which stage does the distinctive trait-selective behavioural expression (trait ``phenotype'') emerge? Phenotype, here refers to output logits-based measures ($B_1$, $B_2$ the intercepts of a regression upon logprob ratios between trait-positive vs -negative answers, defined in \ref{tab:symbols}) rather than CAA activation measures because of the non-availability of a signed effect (see above, App.\ref{app:signed}). We find that the phenotype arises early in SFT, rather than DPO (82\% of the final effect by 0.48 epoch, and saturation by 0.70, see Fig.\ref{fig:oct-sft-curve}A). This is consistent with \citet{maiya2025opencharactertrainingshaping}'s finding of early character-instillation after a single epoch. 

Our contributions are to map the full 3-epoch training trajectory (versus the 1-epoch released artifact in OCT), and apply their pipeline specifically to impulsivity. Importantly, we specifically control for the possibility that SFT just perturbs the model more, and behavioural changes are the unsurprising result of further training. We find (Fig.\ref{fig:oct-sft-curve}D) that, in order to match the functional efficacy of SFT, at a given epoch, we would need a DPO adapter 6-8x higher $s$. This confirms the trait-specificity of SFT, though DPO remains important as the generator of the introspection dataset and SFT-on-a-DPO model increases trait-specificity compared to SFT-on-an-unadapted model (\S\ref{sec:oct-stage}). This result comes with the caveat that it is single-constitution, single-seed, layer 15.

\section{Discussion}
\paragraph{Constitutional character training operates generally across roles.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Constitution $\times$ trait effects are materially larger than constitution $\times$ persona or full triple interactions: $CT/CP \approx 13$ ($0.170$ against $0.013$).
    \item Constitutional differences are therefore an order of magnitude more trait-dependent than persona-dependent.
    \item Tentative interpretation: character training modulates trait representations relatively consistently across role contexts rather than installing a separate context-specific policy for every persona.
    \item Do not convert this directly into a behavioural-generalization claim without output-level evaluation.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Large representational changes aren't inherently interpretable.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A large internal change after meaningful training is weak evidence that the changed direction/geometry represents the training objective.
    \item Translation, global reshaping, functional dose, and non-semantic update structure can all create apparently interpretable geometric signatures.
    \item Methodological implication: semantic claims from post-training representation comparisons need dose-matched and structure-matched controls.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Character training contributes to existing structure rather than creating new entanglements.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The dominant terms are the ones that were already there before any constitution was applied: $T$ $0.372$ and $TP$ $0.175$ together are over half the change, against $CTP$ $0.036$.
    \item Reading: character training scales and shifts a trait$\times$persona structure the base model already has, rather than writing a new persona-specific arrangement on top of it.
    \item This is a statement about where the variance sits, not about mechanism. The decomposition cannot distinguish ``the existing structure is reused'' from ``the perturbation is too small to overwrite it''.
    \item It is also the reading most exposed to the dose confound, since every term above scales with displacement. It should be stated at matched dose or not at all.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{A signed claim is available behaviourally, and only behaviourally.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The representational route to a signed claim failed its validity test (Fig.~\ref{fig:signed}); the behavioural route survives it (Fig.~\ref{fig:logits}).
    \item Both failures have the same shape: generic contraction in the geometry, generic compression toward indifference in the logits. The correction is what makes either readable.
    \item Practical implication: a gain/loss chart built on unvalidated signed projections would have reported the impulsiveness adapter as having made the model \emph{less} impulsive. The validity check is not optional bookkeeping.
    \item The behavioural contrast is also the only place where content and training procedure are separated, because it compares arms within the trained family rather than trained against untrained.
\end{itemize}




\section{Limitations and Next Experiments}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $n=1$ trained adapter for goodness, mathematical and misalignment: semantics cannot be separated from adapter idiosyncrasy for those three. Impulsiveness has two seeds, but at $n=2$ the seed contribution is still not separable from rig nondeterminism.
    \item No sham-trained LoRA. This is the single most load-bearing gap: it is the control that would separate ``constitutional content'' from ``this training procedure'' in Fig.~\ref{fig:directions}, and the control that would settle whether the untrained arms' higher $CTP$ residual is idiosyncrasy or incoherence.
    \item No free-form behavioural evaluation. Fig.~\ref{fig:logits} is a forced choice between two answer letters; nothing in this work generates a completion, so none of it constrains what these arms would actually write.
    \item Stage decomposition is one constitution, one seed, one layer, and its cleaner pair ($M_{D+0.25S}$ vs $M_F$) answers a question about the merge rather than about training stages.
    \item Bootstrap intervals condition on the chosen 8 traits and 10 personas and cover question-sampling uncertainty only; they are not population inference and not uncertainty over training seeds.
    \item Dose uncertainty is not propagated jointly into the matched-dose comparisons.
    \item In order to disambiguate the caveats in \ref{sec:ctp-tiny}, we plan to train on a set of "sham" constitutions (that are linguistically sensically but reduce the constitutionally-relevant content in various ways), using OCT's recipe and hyperparameters, but with the constitutional text. If those produced $CTP$ attributions around 0.03-0.04, this might imply that any LoRA created through the OCT pipeline, even if constitutionally vacuous, would give a significant $CTP$ attribution.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Constitutional character training produces large and structured internal changes, but the largest geometric effects are not uniquely semantic.
    \item The direct triple interaction is small: constitutional effects are mostly lower-order and comparatively uniform across personas.
    \item The practical interpretability lesson is to distinguish what a model \emph{learned} from what merely \emph{happened to its representation space because it was fine-tuned}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Appendix: Decomposing the OCT training pipeline}
\label{sec:oct-stage}

Constitutional training in the OCT recipe \citep{maiya2025opencharactertrainingshaping} has multiple stages: a distillation stage using DPO to produce a LoRA adapted student. This DPO-adapted student then generates a training corpus: from reflections upon, and conversations with other instances of, itself. The introspection corpus is then used for supervised fine-tuning of the student to produce a second LoRA. The DPO and SFT LoRAs are merged, and this is the released artifact.

\paragraph{Why the question is asked at all.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Every result above is measured on the merged OCT artifact, so it is worth knowing where in the pipeline the \texttt{impulsiveness} phenotype actually appears.
    \item The OCT pipeline is DPO, then generation of an introspection corpus \emph{by the DPO model}, then SFT on that corpus, then a weighted adapter merge. Fig.~\ref{fig:oct-pipeline} shows the data flow; the effect could come from any stage, or from the merge implementation itself.
    \item Legend for the model states: M (the unmodified base model, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct); $M_D$ (base plus the DPO adapter; the DPO weight update alone, before any SFT); $M_{D+S}$ (base plus the full DPO and SFT updates; the native post-SFT state, i.e. the SFT adapter applied to the model it was actually trained on); $M_{D+0.25S}$ (base plus the DPO update and a quarter of the SFT update, summed directly in weight space; the merge OCT intended, with no cross terms); $M_F$ (the released-style artifact: peft's \texttt{add\_weighted\_adapter([DPO, SFT], [1, 0.25], "linear")}, which combines the LoRA factors and so equals $M_{D+0.25S}$ plus factor-space cross terms); $M_S$ (a separately trained SFT adapter whose optimisation started from base weights instead of the DPO model, on the identical frozen corpus; DPO is removed from the weights but not from the corpus, which the DPO model generated); $M+A_S$ (the existing SFT adapter (fitted around $M_D$) applied to plain base; an off-base diagnostic, not "SFT alone")
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The raw stage ordering is confounded with dose and carries nothing on its own.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Table~\ref{tab:oct-stage-raw} reports each state on the registered forced-prompt contrast $B_1$ and the geometric endpoints. Read naively it looks like a clean localisation.
    \item It is not. Across the measured states the ordering of $B_1$ is \emph{perfectly} rank-correlated with measured functional dose (Spearman $=+1.000$), so stage and displacement are not separated by that table.
    \item Nominal LoRA scale cannot rescue it. At $s{=}1$ the five constructions already span measured dose $0.54$--$1.11$, the relation is nonlinear within every state ($\mathrm{dose}/s$ spans $\sim\!2\times$), and two states turn over at $s{=}2$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:oct-dose-calibration}).
    \item This table should therefore never be shown as a stage result without that caveat attached. It is orientation only.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{At matched functional dose, both stage differences survive.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Each pair is compared only inside the range of measured dose where \emph{both} members were actually measured (shaded in Fig.~\ref{fig:oct-matched-dose}); nothing is extrapolated, and interpolation is restricted to each state's monotone prefix.
    \item $M_S$ against $M_D$: at matched dose $M_S$ exceeds $M_D$ on the registered endpoint by $8$--$13\times$ ($+2.12$ vs $+0.16$ at dose $0.586$, rising to $+3.11$ vs $+0.37$ at dose $0.817$), and is markedly more selective ($1.59$--$1.76$ against $1.11$--$1.16$).
    \item Reading: at comparable functional displacement, the DPO-generated introspection corpus learned through SFT is a far more potent carrier of the phenotype than the DPO weight update itself.
    \item What that does \emph{not} say: DPO is not shown to be unnecessary. DPO generated the corpus $M_S$ trains on, so removing DPO from the weights does not remove it from the causal history.
    \item Caveat bounding how strongly pair 1 can be phrased: reaching the overlap needs $M_D$ at $s\approx1.4$--$2.0$ and $M_S$ at $s\approx0.5$--$0.7$, so the comparison is between two scaled artifacts, neither of which is a trained state. This is inherent --- $M_D$ is simply the weakest perturbation.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The released merge is not the additive update it is read as.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $M_F$ and $M_{D+0.25S}$ contain the same DPO and SFT components in the same $1{:}0.25$ proportion. They differ only in that peft combines LoRA \emph{factors}, which introduces the cross terms $B_D A_S + B_S A_D$.
    \item The cross terms are surplus rather than a mis-weighting, and this is derived rather than asserted. Extracting the factor coefficients from the released merge gives $A_{\mathrm{merged}}=\sqrt{2}A_D+(1/\sqrt{2})A_S$, and likewise for $B$. With the adapters' $\alpha/r=2$ and the merge's $\alpha/r=1$,
    \[\Delta W_{\mathrm{merged}}=\underbrace{2B_DA_D}_{=\;\Delta W_D}+\underbrace{0.5B_SA_S}_{=\;0.25\,\Delta W_S}+\underbrace{B_DA_S+B_SA_D}_{\text{artifact}}.\]
    \item So the two \emph{intended} terms come out at exactly the nominal $1.0$ and $0.25$: the merge does not mis-weight what OCT asked for, it adds structure OCT did not ask for.
    \item At matched dose those terms roughly double the behavioural contrast: $B_1$ goes from $+0.51$ to $+0.93$ at dose $0.578$, and from $+0.97$ to $+2.00$ at dose $0.842$ (Table~\ref{tab:oct-peft-merge}).
    \item This pair is the cleaner of the two: its overlap sits comfortably inside both states' measured ranges and contains both trained states.
    \item No share of behaviour is attributed to the cross terms. Behaviour is nonlinear in the weight update, so no such share is defined; the $61.9\%$ figure quoted elsewhere is a Frobenius norm ratio in weight space.
    \item Practical consequence: a reconstruction that merges DPO and SFT additively is not the same model as the released artifact, which matters for anyone comparing published OCT adapters against their own.
\end{itemize}

\section{Experimental details \& controls}

\subsection{Broad non-semantic shifts}
\label{app:broad-shift}
\begin{itemize}
    \item In order to control for the possibility that any LoRA, including one devoid of semantic content, would cause a representational shift, we used untrained scrambled LoRA in three ways: random-IID weights, singular-spectrum-matched random directions, and per-module coordinate-permuted trained updates.
    \item \textbf{At L15, the persona-common term accounts for 67--77\% of squared adapter-induced movement across trained arms.} Note: this is an exact partition, not a fit: $dG_{c,t}$ is the persona-mean, so the cross term vanishes and $\mathrm{mean}_p\|dV_{c,t,p}\|^2 = \|dG_{c,t}\|^2 +
    \mathrm{mean}_p\|dV_{c,t,p}-dG_{c,t}\|^2$ identically. This measure is consistent with the decomposition in Fig. \ref{fig:ctp}: averaging over personas removes terms with $p$ index, and the terms that survive ($\mu + C + T + CT$) sum to $73.3\%$ at L15, against a $71.2\%$ grand mean here. These aren't identical since one is a mean of per-cell ratios, the
    other a pooled ratio, so this is a consistency check rather than a restatement.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Global reshaping explains a substantial additional component}
\label{app:global-reshaping}
\begin{itemize}
    \item After persona-centering, \[X_{t,p}=V^{\mathrm{base}}_{t,p}-\bar V^{\mathrm{base}}_t,\qquad Y_{c,t,p}=V_{c,t,p}-\bar V_{c,t},\] we fit a single reduced-rank map $\hat A_c$ across all trait--persona cells, \[\hat A_c=\arg\min_A\sum_{(t,p)\in\mathrm{train}}\|Y_{c,t,p}-A X_{t,p}\|^2.\] On held-out cells this one global map removes roughly 39--54\% of the squared base-to-adapted discrepancy, depending on arm. A restricted orthogonal map (rotation/reflection only) explains substantially less.
    \item This suggests that a substantial component of the residual change is a
    global reshaping of the representation space---including scaling/shearing---rather
    than a collection of independently learned trait$\times$persona changes.
    \item Interpretation: much of the remaining base-to-adapted difference has a globally systematic linear form, reducing how much change needs a cell-specific semantic explanation.
    \item This signature is not diagnostic of training either, and fails the same control as the others. In Fig.~\ref{fig:diagnostics}B the untrained arms sit at the \emph{top} of the linear-map column --- \texttt{random\_perm} $0.561$, \texttt{random\_spec} $0.513$, \texttt{random\_iid} $0.488$ --- at or above every trained arm ($0.388$--$0.541$). A large fraction of centered change being globally linear is a property of being perturbed, not of being trained.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Relative persona geometry is strongly dose-dependent}
\label{app:rdm}
\begin{itemize}
    \item In Fig.~\ref{fig:rdm-dose}A, within-arm dose ladders causally show that RDM preservation falls as functional dose rises. This means that the pairwise distances in the $10\times10$ persona-conditioned trait vector for each trait increases. That is, RDM of 1 means persona pairs that were relatively close in the unadapted model are still relatively close (in the adapted), and pairs that were relatively far apart are still relatively far apart.
    \item This means that simply perturbing the model more strongly causes progressively more distortion of the existing persona geometry.
    \item At matched functional dose (Fig.~\ref{fig:rdm-dose}C), goodness and impulsiveness follow essentially the same RDM-preservation curve ($0.834$, $0.826$); mathematical sits above both ($0.878$) and misalignment below them ($0.768$).
    \item \textbf{That ordering is not interpretable as constitutional semantics, because the untrained arms straddle the whole trained range: \texttt{random\_iid} preserves geometry \emph{better} than every trained arm ($0.886$) and \texttt{random\_perm} preserves it \emph{worse} than misalignment ($0.761$). Misalignment is not the outlier; it is inside a band that untrained perturbations also occupy.}
    \item Estimator note: panels C/D dose-correct \emph{every} arm, whereas \S7.7 of the underlying results write-up corrects only the random arms and quotes the trained arms at their measured doses. This changes the printed ordering (misalignment rises from $0.732$ to $0.768$) but not the claim, which it slightly strengthens: untrained spread $0.125$ against trained spread $0.110$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{ANOVA details}
\label{app:anova}
It is well known in fields using statistical methods (e.g. neuroscience and machine learning) that when trying to estimate a variable's norm/variance from observational data, we must control for the fact that quadratic estimates of noisy data are systematically biased upwards. Following \citep{WaltherEtAl2016,WuEtAl2026}, one method of doing this is a split-batch cross-validated sampling-noise-debiasing. The basic idea is to split the noisy observational data into non-overlapping batches, estimate the relevant norm/variance independently on these disjoint subsets and replace the squared norm/variance of a single estimate (over the entire observations) with the cross-product of the two estimates (on the batches).

Concretely, in the simplified case, let the true vector-valued effect be
$\theta$, and let an estimate from one sample be

\[
\hat{\theta}^{A} = \theta + \epsilon_A,
\]

where $\epsilon_A$ is zero-mean sampling error. A naive estimate of the
squared norm of the effect is

\[
\left\|\hat{\theta}^{A}\right\|_2^2.
\]

Expanding algebraically,

\[
\left(\theta + \epsilon_A\right)^\top
\left(\theta + \epsilon_A\right),
\]

which gives

\[
\theta^\top\theta
+ 2\theta^\top\epsilon_A
+ \epsilon_A^\top\epsilon_A.
\]

Taking expectations,

\[
\mathbb{E}\left[
\left\|\hat{\theta}^{A}\right\|_2^2
\right]
=
\|\theta\|_2^2
+ 2\theta^\top\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_A]
+ \mathbb{E}\left[\epsilon_A^\top\epsilon_A\right].
\]

Assuming the sampling error has zero mean,
$\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_A]=0$, the linear error term vanishes, but the
quadratic noise term does not:

\[
\mathbb{E}\left[
\left\|\hat{\theta}^{A}\right\|_2^2
\right]
=
\|\theta\|_2^2
+
\operatorname{tr}\left(\Sigma_{\epsilon}\right),
\]

where

\[
\Sigma_{\epsilon}
=
\operatorname{Cov}(\epsilon_A).
\]

The split-batch debiasing method involves splitting the observations into two disjoint samples, producing two independent estimates,

\[
\hat{\theta}^{A} = \theta + \epsilon_A,
\qquad
\hat{\theta}^{B} = \theta + \epsilon_B.
\]

We estimate the squared norm using the cross-product

\[
\left(\hat{\theta}^{A}\right)^\top
\hat{\theta}^{B}.
\]

Expanding and taking expectations,

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}\left[
\left(\hat{\theta}^{A}\right)^\top
\hat{\theta}^{B}
\right]
&=
\theta^\top\theta
+ \theta^\top\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_B]
+ \mathbb{E}[\epsilon_A]^\top\theta
+ \mathbb{E}\left[\epsilon_A^\top\epsilon_B\right].
\end{aligned}
\]

If the errors are zero-mean and independent across the two disjoint
samples,

\[
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_A]
=
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_B]
=
0,
\qquad
\mathbb{E}\left[\epsilon_A^\top\epsilon_B\right]
=
0,
\]

and therefore

\[
\mathbb{E}\left[
\left(\hat{\theta}^{A}\right)^\top
\hat{\theta}^{B}
\right]
=
\theta^\top\theta
=
\|\theta\|_2^2.
\]

In this debiased example, the single-bias terms are zero (in expectation), but crucially, the cross product of bias terms from two non-overlapping estimates is \textit{also} zero in expectation, giving an estimate of $\theta$ that is unbiased.

In the present context, we have C=4,T=8,P=10,H=4096 corresponding to constitution, trait, persona/role, and residual stream size, and 500 questions. The data are split into 40 repetitions: disjoint batches of 250 questions each (repetitions reuse questions in different random partitions). The relevant code is here: \provlink{scripts/caa_three_way_interaction.py}.


\subsection{Untrained perturbations reproduce broad contraction}
\begin{itemize}
    \item At ordinary weight scale, untrained LoRAs have minimal effect (in activation differences to unadapted model); the geometric intuition is that trained adapters are more potent owing to vector directions that are already partially aligned.
    \item In order to functionally match doses (i.e. equalize the difference in activations to a character-trained model at a given layer), we need an adaptor application weight $s = 16--19$ to get the same effect as a trained OCT adaptor at $s = 1$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:dose-calibration}B: CAA answer-token displacement $0.545$ at \texttt{random\_iid} $s{=}16$, $0.575$ at \texttt{random\_spec} $s{=}19$, $0.598$ at \texttt{random\_perm} $s{=}16$, against $0.560$ for goodness at $s{=}1$).
    \item The size of that gap is itself the reason nominal scale cannot be used as the comparison variable. At $s{=}1$ an untrained LoRA is essentially inert: neutral-prompt KL is $0.0012$ (\texttt{random\_iid}) and $0.0008$ (\texttt{random\_spec}) against $0.606$ for goodness, roughly $500$--$700\times$ less (Fig.~\ref{fig:dose-calibration}A).
    \item At so matched functional dose, all three untrained constructions reproduce most of the broad dispersion contraction, and the spread in RDM preservation among untrained controls is at least as large as the spread among trained constitutions (Fig.~\ref{fig:rdm-dose}C,D).
    \item Another comparison (Fig.~\ref{fig:directions}) is the cosine similarity of persona-mean representational shift (at matched functional dose) between two adapted models (either OCT LoRA or a randomised/untrained LoRA), where \[dG_{c,t}=\frac{1}{P}\sum_{p=1}^{P}dV_{c,t,p}.\] We find that character-trained models' representational shifts are directionally more similar to each other, than they are to untrained models' shifts.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{A signed effect survives behaviourally, though not geometrically}
\label{app:signed}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fig.~\ref{fig:signed} shows that no validated \emph{signed} statement can be made from the representations: projecting the persona-common shift onto the base-model trait axis fails its own validity checks for trained adapters and passes for the randomized control.
    \item Fig.~\ref{fig:logits} asks the same question of the model's outputs rather than its activations, reading the log-odds of a trait-positive over a trait-negative answer on forced-choice CAA items.
    \item The naive shift is invalid as a preference measure. Every arm compresses preferences toward indifference (retention $k=0.02$--$0.29$ trained, $0.68$--$0.81$ untrained), so the raw change is close to a mirror image of wherever the base model already stood (Fig.~\ref{fig:logits}A,B).
    \item Correcting for compression means reporting the fitted preference at base indifference rather than the raw shift. This is interpolation, not extrapolation: $9$--$21\%$ of items per trait sit within $|\text{log-odds}_{\text{base}}|<1$.
    \item The correction does not rest on the fitted shape. Replacing the affine fit with a polarity-balanced mean over items near indifference --- no shape model at all --- moves the contrast by at most $0.14$. That model-free check, not the goodness of the linear fit, is what the result stands on.
    \item Corrected, the impulsiveness and misalignment arms move specifically toward impulsivity ($+3.16$, $+2.52$) and risk-taking ($+2.45$, $+2.37$); the trait-vs-others contrast is $+2.08$ and $+2.49$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:logits}C,D).
    \item Goodness ($-0.39$), mathematical ($-0.36$) and both untrained arms (intervals covering zero: $-0.12$, $-0.14$) do not. The ordering is the same under the default CAA prompt.
    \item This is the one comparison in the paper not exposed to the content-versus-procedure confound of Fig.~\ref{fig:directions}. Goodness, mathematical and impulsiveness share pipeline, rank, initialisation and corpus shape, and sit at almost identical compression ($k=0.25$, $0.28$, $0.29$), yet score $-0.39$, $-0.36$ and $+2.08$. Procedure is held constant; only the constitution differs.
    \item Scope limit: this is a forced choice between two answer letters, not free-form behaviour. Nothing here generates a completion, and the result should not be extrapolated to open-ended output without a generation study.
\end{itemize}
